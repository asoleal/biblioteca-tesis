Substrate moisture content is an important but not well-understood variable in production and waste
reduction processes that involves black soldier ﬂy (BSF) larvae. The purpose of this paper is to charac-
terise growth and metabolic performance of BSF larvae on substrate moisture contents from 45 to 85%.
Larvae developed into prepupae only at 45–75% substrate moisture content. Within this interval, the
maximal speciﬁc growth rate was highest (0.73 day1), the growth period shortest (13 days), and the
maximal dry weight lowest (88 mg) at 45% moisture content. Differences in cost of growth and mainte-
nance were not observed at the different substrate moisture contents, and differences in larval perfor-
mance were likely associated to differences in co-occurring microbial activities. As much as 22% of the
substrate carbon was emitted as CO2 at 45% moisture content by microorganisms, measured as the dif-
ference between total respiration and larval respiration, whereas microbial CO2 production amounted to
only 3% of the substrate carbon at 75% moisture content. As consequence of the high speciﬁc growth rate
and short growth phase, the overall net growth efﬁciency was higher at 45% moisture content (0.62) than
at 75% moisture content (0.52). Overall, the metabolic performance of the BSF larvae was insensitive to
differences in substrate moisture content. Their performance was, however indirectly affected by the sub-
strate moisture content due to differences in co-occurring microbial processes in the substrate.

1. Introduction
The black soldier ﬂy (BSF, Hermetia illucens) is a promising
insect species for upcycling organic waste into high quality bio-
mass (Gold et al., 2020). BSF larvae are able to convert a wide range
of organic materials into larval biomass rich in proteins and lipids,
and are well suited for use as animal feed (Makkar et al., 2014).
Experimental studies on BSF cultivation and waste processing vary
considerably in design (Bosch et al., 2019), and basic characterisa-
tions of how BSF larvae respond to differences in key rearing con-
ditions may enhance opportunities to compare and evaluate
experimental results across studies.
The moisture content in substrates is an important variable in
processes involving BSF larvae. Fatchurochim et al. (1989) showed
that BSF larvae grow and develop into pupae at substrate moisture
contents of 30–70% (based on weight). Other studies suggest that
substrate moisture contents in the range of 50–80% are optimal
for larval development, ﬁnal weight, feed conversion efﬁciency,
and yield (Cheng et al., 2017; Cammack and Tomberlin, 2017;
Palma et al., 2018; Abduh et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2019). BSF larvae
have even been grown at substrate moisture contents of 90–97.5%,
but with lower survival rates and reduced body weights than at
75–88% substrate moisture (Lalander et al., 2020). The substrate
moisture content inﬂuences substrate texture and affects larval
movement, feed consumption, growth and survival (Makkar
et al., 2014; Palma et al., 2018). Variations in microbial activity
have also been linked to differences in substrate moisture content
(Chen et al., 2019). High microbial activities in substrates with low
substrate moisture contents may be associated with high porosity
and favourable oxygen transfer rates (Palma et al., 2018).
The aim of this study is thus to characterise growth and meta-
bolic performance of BSF larvae at different substrate moisture
contents, in terms of speciﬁc rates of growth, CO2 production,
and feed assimilation, as well as cost of growth, maintenance,
and net growth efﬁciency (NGE).

2. Materials and methods
2.1. Cultivation
BSF eggs were supplied by Entomass Aps (Løkken, Denmark). The
eggs (0.2–0.3 g) were placed on top and hatched in nurseries made of
cylindrical plastic containers (radius = 5.5 cm, height = 13 cm) con-
taining 100 g (91% dry weight) of a commercial chicken feed
(Kyllekræs 3, Danish Agro, Karise, Denmark) and 150 g water. The
nurseries were placed at 26.5–28.0 C and 23–40 %RH. After 9 days,
the larvae reached approximately 0.5 mg dry weight (DW) and were
named starter larvae (Parodi et al., 2020). Cultivation experiments
were carried out in the same containers, each supplemented with
400–600 g of chicken feed (190–225 g DW), and with moisture con-
tents of 45, 55, 65, 75, and 85%, respectively (Table 1). On Day 0 of the
cultivation experiments, each container was inoculated with 75–
188 starter larvae and 1.2 g of substrate DW per larvae. The amount
of substrate and number of starter larvae were adjusted in order to
minimise variations between initial substrate mass and substrate
height (4.8–6.2 cm), and larval density (0.76–2 cm2). Five replicate
cultures were conducted for each treatment. The cultures were
placed at 26.5–28.0 C and 23–40 %RH until the larvae developed
into prepupae. Surviving prepupae were counted on Day 19 (28 days
after the eggs were placed in the nurseries).
2.2. CO2 production in BSF larvae
CO2 production rates were measured on a daily basis on 5 ran-
domly selected larvae from each container. The larvae were
washed in 28 C water to remove substrate residues from their sur-
face and then transferred to an airtight 50 mL plastic centrifugation
tube placed in an aluminium heating block (Hettich Benelux model
MHR-23) at 28 C. A pre-calibrated wireless CO2 Sensor PS-3208
(Pasco, Roseville, California) was inserted into the top of the tube
and CO2 production rates were determined from the linear increase
in CO2 concentration in the tube. Measurements were completed
in 5 min, including 2 min for the sensor to stabilise (Laganaro
et al., 2021).
2.3. Dry weight content of BSF larvae
After larval CO2 production rates had been quantiﬁed, the wet
weight of the 5 randomly selected larvae was measured on an
electronic scale before they were returned alive to their original
container. The dry weight of another 3 randomly selected larvae
from each container was measured every third day after drying
at 95 C until constant weight. Subsequent estimates of overall res-
piration rates and mortality did not include these larvae.
2.4. Microbial CO2 production
The overall CO2 emission rates from BSF larval culture, includ-
ing larval as well as microbial CO2 production, were measured in
the headspaces above the substrate using a pre-calibrated wireless
CO2 Sensor PS-3208. The containers were closed by airtight lids
during measurements, which were completed in less than 3 min.
The CO2 emission rates were quantiﬁed as described in Section 2.2
and microbial CO2 production was estimated as the difference
between the overall CO2 emission rate and larval CO2 production
rate (calculated as the product of individual CO2 production rates
and the number of larvae in each culture).
2.5. Water holding capacity
The water holding capacity (WHC) of the chicken feed was
determined in 5 replicate trials. Chicken feed and water were
mixed to a water content above 80% (based on weight) for 24 h
in closed containers to ensure water saturation of the chicken feed,
and ﬁnally transferred to a pre-wetted and pre-weighed coffee ﬁl-
ter. Excess water was allowed to drain and the WHC was deter-
mined from the increase in mass of drained chicken feed
compared to its original dry mass.
2.6. Moisture content in substrate
Controlled amounts of water were added to each container
daily, in order to maintain constant substrate moisture contents
in the substrates (Supplementary information, Eqs. S1-S4). Firstly,
the mass of each container was measured, and the mass of the
wet substrate was estimated by subtracting the mass of the BSF
larvae. Secondly, it was estimated how much substrate (dry
weight) that had been lost via respiration by summing up the mea-
sured overall CO2 production rates and the amounts of substrate
incorporated into larval biomass. The remaining substrate dry
weight was then estimated as the difference between the initial
and metabolised substrate dry weights. The substrate moisture

2.8. Conversion factors and statistical analysis
In order to compare measurements of growth and CO2 produc-
tion, measurements were expressed as speciﬁc rates based on the
mass of carbon in the larvae, in substrates, and in CO2. We assumed
that carbon made up 49% of the organic fraction of the larvae,
which is the carbon content in biomass of average elementary
composition (Roels, 1980). The organic fraction of the larvae was
estimated from measurements of the dry weight of the larvae,
assuming 5% ash content (Laganaro et al., 2021). We also assumed
that the carbon content of chicken feed was 49% of the organic
fraction (94.8% of the dry weight).
Maximal weights and maximal speciﬁc growth rates of BSF lar-
vae grown at different substrate moisture contents were compared
by a Tukey test on results from one-way ANOVA in Excel 2016. A
preliminary study had veriﬁed that individual larval dry weight
followed Gaussian distributions throughout the growth phase.

3.1. Cultivation conditions
The WHC of the chicken feed was 70 ± 0.2% (n = 5). Substrate
moisture contents of 45–85% thus corresponded to 64–121%,
respectively of the WHC (Table 1) and affected culture conditions.
In between daily samplings, clumps developed at the surface of
substrates with 45% moisture content and most larvae were
observed in the bottom half. Substrates with 55% and 65% moisture
contents developed a crust at the surface and larvae were found at
all depths. Substrates with 75% moisture content had a thin water
ﬁlm at the surface and larvae were predominantly observed in the
upper half. Substrates with 85% moisture content had a water
phase at the surface and all larvae died. BSF larvae have survived
higher moisture contents in other substrates (Lalander et al.
2020), maybe due to differences in WHC. Since the substrates
became stratiﬁed, substrate moisture contents could not be
assayed from sampling during the growth period. Instead, water
was replenished based on a theoretical approach (Supplementary
information, Fig. S1). The substrate moisture content measured
on Day 19, when the cultures were terminated, had remained close
to the expected levels (Table 1).
The BSF larvae grew and developed into prepupae in substrates
with 45–75% moisture content (Fig. 1A, C, E, G) and reached 299–
403 mg wet weight (WW, Table 1). Prepupae commonly weighs
55–299 mg WW depending on substrate (see e.g. Gold et al.,
2018; Lalander et al., 2019) but weights up to at least 373 mg have
been seen (Lalander et al., 2020). The high weight of the prepupae
in this study indicates that suitable growth conditions had been
established. On Day 19, when cultures were harvested, 91 ± 4,
89 ± 4, 95 ± 10, and 81 ± 11% of the larvae were alive as prepupae
at 45, 55, 65, and 75% moisture content, respectively. Maximal
weights were reached on Day 13–19, earliest in the driest
substrates (Table 1). Fatchurochim et al. (1989) also observed that
BSF larvae grew for longer periods in wet compared to more dry
substrates. Growth followed sigmoidal curves with exponential
phases of 8–12 days (Supplementary information, Fig. S2). The lar-
vae grew larger at 65% and 75% substrate moisture content (104–
114 mg DW) than at 45% and 55% substrate moisture content
(84–90 mg DW, p = 2.7  10-9). The relationship between dry
weight content and larval size is shown in Supplementary informa-
tion, Fig. S3). The maximal speciﬁc growth rate was, in contrast,
higher at 45% (0.73 day1) compared to 75% substrate moisture
content (0.52 day1), p = 2.8  10-6. These values fall within the
wide interval of maximal speciﬁc growth rates on different sub-
strates (0.05–1.08 day1) that previously have been estimated by
ﬁtting sigmoidal curves to larval body widths (Sripontan et al.,
2020). Speciﬁc growth rates estimated from ln-transformed larval
weights conﬁrmed that highest value occurred at lowest substrate
moisture content (Supplementary information, Fig. S2). Highest
speciﬁc growth rates were thereby found at substrate moisture
contents below levels that in other studies have favoured growth
and development of BSF larvae (Cheng et al., 2017; Cammack
and Tomberlin, 2017; Palma et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2019).

3.2. Microbial activities
The substrate moisture content strongly affected the microbial
CO2 production in the substrates (Fig. 1B, D, F, H). At 45% and
55% substrate moisture contents, the overall CO2 emission vastly
exceeded larval CO2 production (Table 2). Microbial processes con-
verted 12–22% of the substrate carbon into CO2, thereby making
this fraction unavailable for the BSF larvae. At 65% and 75% sub-
strate moisture content, the BSF larvae dominated the CO2 emis-
sions. Microbial CO2 emissions peaked already after 3–4 days at
45–55% substrate moisture content. The lower microbial CO2 emis-
sion rates at the end of the cultivations (Fig. 1B and D) suggest that
the most readily available feed compounds had been consumed
within the ﬁrst week. Similarly during composting processes the
soluble and most available fractions may be degraded within less
than a week, giving rise to decreasing oxygen uptake rates (Xi
et al., 2005). Thus, microbial activities therefore affected the feed
value of the remaining substrate in late growth phases. Parodi
et al. (2020) also observed a peak in CO2 emissions 4 days after
BSF cultures had been started, where after the emission rates
decreased. The shorter growth phase and lower maximal weight
of prepupae in the driest substrates (Table 1) were likely results
of microbial deterioration of substrate availability and quality. It
should be noted that CO2 emissions into culture headspaces repre-
sent only a fraction of the microbial activities. Some CO2 may
remain dissolved in pore water and microorganisms may convert
substrate components into volatile fermentation products. Indeed,
culture mass balances from Day 0 to Day 19 (Supplementary infor-
mation, Table S1) conﬁrmed that the CO2 measurements did not
fully explain the overall decrease in substrate weight after drying.
Cumulative CO2 emissions and larval growth summed up to 87% of
the removed substrate at 45% substrate moisture content and only
45% at 75% substrate moisture content. A similar result (just 47–
75% carbon removal via CO2 and larval biomass) can be deduced
from Pang et al. (2020), who found that 5–17% of the carbon ini-
tially present in substrates made from pig manure and corncob
was lost via routes different from CO2 (9–12%), CH4 (less than
0.2%), and larval biomass (4–7%).
3.3. Metabolic performance
The carbon content in the BSF larvae, the cumulative amount of
carbon emitted as CO2, and the accumulated amount of carbon
assimilated by the larvae is shown in Fig. 2A, C, E, G. At the time
the larvae reached maximal weight, corresponding to 39–52 mg
C (Table 1), each larva had assimilated feed corresponding to 72–
91 mg C, resulting in NGEavg between 0.52 and 0.62 (Table 1). High-
est NGEavg was in the larvae reared at lowest substrate moisture
content. Gold et al. (2020) found that carbon made up 62% of the
organic fraction of BSF prepupae. Such high level will hardly be
reached in growing larvae due to their lower lipid contents, but
if so, NGEavg estimates would have been slightly higher (0.56–
0.65) compared with our results using 49% carbon content. In all
circumstances, the high NGEavg at low substrate moisture contents
was a consequence of the high speciﬁc growth rates and short
growth phases (Table 1) since the larvae spend more substrate
for maintenance purposes the longer the growth phase was.
Speciﬁc rates of growth, CO2 production, and feed assimilation
were highest in the smallest larvae and decreased as the larvae
approached maximal weight (Fig. 2A, C, E, G). The speciﬁc growth
rate and thus the speciﬁc feed assimilation rate reached highest
values at 45% substrate moisture content. From Day 2, when the
larvae had grown larger than 1 mg DW, and until growth stopped,
the speciﬁc CO2 production rate became linearly correlated to the
speciﬁc growth rate at 45% and 55% substrate moisture content
(Fig. 2B and D), as predicted by Eq. (3). At 65% and 75% substrate
moisture content, the relationship between the speciﬁc rates of
CO2 production and growth became linear only after Day 4 and
Day 5, when the larvae were larger than 5 and 6 mg DW, respec-
tively. Until then, measured speciﬁc CO2 production rates were
inexplicable high and we cannot rule out that respiration measure-
ments on the smallest larvae may have been affected by so-called
‘ﬂask effects’ (Wightman, 1981). These larvae are fragile and the
separation of larvae from substrates during respiration measure-
ments may have been stressful. However, only minor parts of the
biomass (1–6%) were produced these ﬁrst days of growth and the
estimated NGEavg values (Table 1) can potentially have been
affected only little.
The cost of growth, Y = 0.38 and maintenance, m = 0.05 day1
were found by simultaneously ﬁtting Eq. (5) to the estimated
NGE values in all cultures, during the parts of the growth phases
where the speciﬁc CO2 production rate depended linearly on the
speciﬁc growth rate (Fig. 2B, D, F, H). More than 90% of the biomass
was produced during these phases. These values described well the
linear relationships between the speciﬁc rates of CO2 production
and growth (Eq. (3)) as well as the non-linear relationships
between NGE and speciﬁc growth rate at all substrate moisture
contents. Similar cost of growth and maintenance were also found
by Laganaro et al. (2021). Thereby cost of growth and maintenance
metabolism seemed not to be affected by substrate moisture con-
tent, maybe with the exception of larvae smaller than 1–6 mg DW.
Key processes, which affected larval performances in this study are
depicted in Supplementary information, Fig. S4 and did include lar-
val as well as microbial substrate utilisation. Particularly the latter
were affected by substrate moisture content.
4. Conclusion
The results presented in this paper suggest that the metabolic
performance in terms of cost of growth and maintenance in BSF
larvae is insensitive to differences in substrate moisture content
within a broad interval from 45 to 75% (64–107% of the WHC). This
however, do not mean that BSF larvae will perform the same at all
substrate moisture contents. In this study, low substrate moisture
contents favoured microbial substrate degradation to CO2, result-
ing in comparatively short growth phases and small prepupae
but efﬁcient conversion of assimilated substrate into larval bio-
mass. The latter was also strengthened by the high speciﬁc growth
rates observed at low substrate moisture contents. The insensitiv-
