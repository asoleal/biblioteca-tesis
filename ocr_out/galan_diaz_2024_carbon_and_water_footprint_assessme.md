A B S T R A C T  
A comprehensive investigation into the carbon and water footprint of a black soldier fly (Hermetia 
illucens) farm in Murcia, Spain, was carried out following ISO 14064-1 standards. The study, 
utilizing data from BioflyTech, the managing company, spanned various departments to 
comprehensively analyze production and consumption. The results highlight that the carbon 
footprint associated with fresh larva production is approximately thirty times lower than that of 
conventional animal farming, aligning with sustainable practices and offering a promising 
alternative in the quest for eco-friendly protein sources. Moreover, the water footprint of the 
black soldier fly farm is significantly lower than that of traditional animal farms, underscoring the 
environmental advantages of this innovative approach to larva production over conventional 
livestock farming. These valuable insights contribute substantively to the ongoing discourse on 
sustainable and responsible agricultural practices, paving the way for more environmentally 
friendly solutions in the ever-evolving landscape of sustainable food production practices.   
1. Introduction 
The concept of carrying capacity, as defined by Cohen, refers to the number of individuals in a population that can be sustained by 
the resources of a given habitat, representing the asymptote or plateau in the logistic and other sigmoid equations used to model 
population growth (Cohen, 1995). Despite the challenges in calculating this capacity, some authors, such as Romero-Oliva, estimate 
that it may fall within a range of 6000 to 15,000 million inhabitants, with an average of about 10,000 million inhabitants (Romer­
o-Oliva, 2023). This scenario is rapidly approaching, according to the UN’s projections for the year 2050, where the global population 
is expected to reach 10 billion inhabitants (Nations, 2019). 
This sharp population increase raises significant concerns about food supply for both humans and animals. The primary source of 
protein in diets is of animal origin, posing a sustainability challenge for the planet. Livestock production is estimated to occupy 30% of 
the world’s land - excluding frozen surfaces - or 75% of agricultural land, and it consumes approximately 8% of global human water use 
((FAO (Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations), 2009). 
Additionally, it should be noted that over 14% of the total CO2 emitted into the atmosphere corresponds to this sector - 7.1 Gigatons 
of CO2-equivalent per year (Gerber et al., 2013). 
When examining global averages, it becomes apparent that the water footprint of meat increases incrementally from chicken meat 
(4300 m3/t), goat meat (5500 m3/t), pig meat (6000 m3/t), and sheep meat (10,400 m3/t) to beef (15,400 m3/t). These variations can

Regarding water footprint analysis, Mekonnen et al. (Mekonnen and Hoekstra, 2012) highlight that beef cattle have the largest

reveal more nuances about their specific impact on the global water footprint. 
Due to all the aforementioned factors, it becomes of vital importance to find a protein source that has high nutritional value while 
also being environmentally friendly in terms of greenhouse gas emissions and water consumption. Insect rearing for protein production 
is a viable alternative both economically and environmentally. The speed at which insects reach maturity and the number of offspring 
generated by each individual is much higher than that of any farm animal. 
The use of insects in the human diet is common in some parts of the planet, such as Asia, Latin America, or Africa (Makkar et al., 
2014). Recently, the European Commission has approved the consumption of the house cricket (Acheta domesticus) (European 
Parliament and the Council of the European Union, 2011). However, Europeans are still hesitant to include insects in their regular diet. 
Indeed, insect larvae, such as the black soldier fly (H. illucens), have been increasingly used to produce flour and feed for animal 
nutrition due to their nutritional qualities. Barragan-Fonseca et al. (Barragan-Fonseca, Dicke and van Loon, 2018) investigated the 
effects of nutrient concentration in the diet and larval density on the performance and body composition of Hermetia illucens larvae. In 
their study, two feeding regimes were employed: one visually adjusted and another with fixed ad libitum ration. They found high larval 
survival rates under both regimes (approximately 95%), yet larval density and nutrient concentration significantly influenced 
development time and individual larval weight. For instance, larvae reached an average weight of 0.18 ± 0.03 g when reared at a 
density of 250 larvae per container with a high-protein, high-fat diet. Furthermore, body composition analysis revealed these larvae 
had higher protein content (55.7 ± 0.9% dry weight) and fat content (32.4 ± 1.0% dry weight). These findings underscore the 
importance of optimizing diet nutrient quality and managing larval density to enhance both the performance and nutritional quality of 
H. illucens larvae, critical for their potential application in large-scale food and feed production systems. In addition to its nutritional 
value, potential medical applications of Hermetia illucens have also been investigated. Guarnier et al. (Guarnieri et al., 2022) explored 
the antimicrobial properties of chitosan derived from larvae, pupal exuviae, and deceased adults of this species. Using 
Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy, they confirmed the structural similarity of the produced chitosan to commercial counterparts 
and found it exhibits significant antimicrobial activity. These findings open new avenues for utilizing this biopolymer in various 
biological and medical fields. 
Several recent studies have assessed the feasibility of substituting black soldier fly larvae meal (BSFLM) for fish meal (FM) in broiler 
diets. Stejskal et al. (2023) found that incorporating BSFLM up to 18% did not affect growth parameters in European perch. Marin et al. 
(2023), through a meta-analysis of 18 studies involving 4229 broiler chickens, concluded that diets containing up to 15% BSFLM 
showed no detrimental effects on growth rates. Nampijja et al. (2023) investigated higher inclusion levels and determined that 
substituting up to 500 g/kg of BSFLM reduced feed intake, improved feed conversion ratio (FCR), and maintained weight gain in 
broilers. They found optimal substitution at 540 g/kg based on FCR, despite slight decreases in muscle protein and Omega-3 fatty acids. 
These studies collectively suggest that replacing FM with BSFLM up to 540 g/kg is viable, providing economic benefits without 
compromising broiler growth or meat quality (Nampijja et al., 2023). 
In this paper, an environmental study of a black soldier fly farm for the production of meal, fat, and compost located in the Region 
of Murcia (Spain) is conducted. It should be noted that several studies have been conducted on the environmental impact of Hermetia 
illucens: Mertenat et al. (2023) (Mertenat et al., 2019) explored the potential for mitigating greenhouse gas emissions through black 
soldier fly (BSF) larval waste treatment in Indonesia, comparing it with open windrow composting. Using a life cycle assessment (LCA) 
approach, they demonstrated that direct CO2eq emissions at the BSF facility were significantly lower compared to composting, being 
up to 47 times lower. The study identified that the main contributors to global warming potential (GWP) were post-composting 
residues (69%) and electricity consumption (up to 55%), highlighting the potential substitution of fish meal with BSF larvae meal 
as a strategy to reduce GWP by up to 30%. 
Wancheng Pang et al. (Pang et al., 2020) investigated the bioconversion of food waste by BSF larvae, assessing how different pH 
conditions influenced carbon and nitrogen conversion efficiency, as well as emissions of gases such as CO2, NH3, CH4 and N2O. Their 
findings underscored that higher pH in substrates enhances larval production and leads to significant reductions in greenhouse gas 
emissions, particularly CH4 and N2O. This study highlights the potential of BSF treatment not only for sustainable waste management 
but also as an effective climate mitigation strategy in urban contexts of low and middle-income countries. 
Besides, Kofi Armah Boakye-Yiadom et al. (Boakye-Yiadom et al., 2022) conducted a comprehensive review focusing on evaluating 
the greenhouse gas emissions and conducting a life cycle assessment (LCA) of black soldier fly (BSF) larvae biotreatment activities. 
Their study underscores the environmental performance of BSF-derived products in mitigating challenges related to feed and organic 
waste management. The review discusses various gas sensing techniques used to quantify GHG and ammonia emissions during BSF 
bioconversion processes, emphasizing the need for standardized guidelines to enhance methodological approaches in impact 
assessments. 
The study has been carried out following the most recent methodologies for calculating carbon footprint and water footprint in 
order to obtain reliable and realistic results.

2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Description of the farm and production cycle 
The company that manages the black soldier fly farm is BioflyTech (hereinafter referred to as BT), located in the Region of Murcia 
(Spain), in a facility covering an area of 21,000 m2. The company BT provides its customers with protein flours, functional fats, and 
enriched compost. All these products are made from the breeding of dipteran insects of Hermetia illucens. 
The productive cycle of the black soldier fly on the farm consists of four stages: 
Rearing stage (Hatchery): Part of the larvae from the eggs of Hermetia illucens will be used to generate new adult flies, which, in turn, 
will lay the eggs that will allow the maintenance of the black soldier fly production process. 
Fattening-Dehydration Stage: The majority of the neonates are allocated to larvae production. These larvae (those that were not used 
in the previous stage) undergo a fattening process, which is the most critical part of the production. Additionally, the option of drying 
and freezing some of the larvae is made possible in this stage. 
Rendering Stage: Upon reaching the expected final weight (0.1–0.11 g per unit) of the larvae, the fattening phase is completed. 
During this rendering stage, the final products, such as flour and fat, are obtained from the dried larvae. 
Post-Production and Sales Stage: In this stage, the products are packaged for sale and stored until they are effectively shipped to 
customers. 
In each of these stages, the company BT obtains various products aimed at their commercialization:  
1. Products from the Hatchery Stage:  
a. Hermetia illucens eggs  
b. Hermetia illucens larvae (neonates)  
c. Puparium fertilizer. The fertilizer is a product derived from the mixture of the substrate (where larvael fattening takes place, 
essentially feed) with the larvael waste until they transition into the adult stage. This fertilizer, which includes the remains of the 
puparium, has a higher added value  
2. Products in the Fattening Stage:  
a. Fresh Hermetia illucens larvae  
b. Frozen Hermetia illucens larvae  
c. Dried Hermetia illucens larvae  
d. Fertilizer. This product does not contain remains of the puparium; it is a byproduct derived from the larvael fattening substrate

2.2. Carbon footprint 
The methodology for calculating Greenhouse Gas (GHG) emissions at BT is based on ISO 14064-1 standard and considers the 
following aspects: Principles of GHG accounting and reporting; determination of organizational boundaries; determination of oper­
ational boundaries; tracking emissions over time; and identification and calculation of GHG emissions. 
According to ISO 14064-1, the following scopes can be distinguished: 
Scope 1: Direct GHG emissions. Scope 1 emissions are considered emissions from sources that are owned or controlled by the 
company. 
Scope 2: Indirect GHG emissions associated with electricity and heat. Scope 2 emissions are indirect emissions resulting from the 
company’s activities but occur from sources not owned or controlled by the company. 
Scope 3: Other indirect emissions. 
The company BT compiles its emissions through the control approach, accounting for 100% of the GHG emissions attributable to 
the operations of the process over which it exercises control. Regarding operational boundaries, it is noted that the company accounts 
for at least scopes 1 and 2 separately. 
In BT, indirect GHG emissions originating from electricity generation have been quantified. The electricity consumption is pro­
portionally allocated across all stages of the studied process. Additionally, the substrate-feed used for the breeding and fattening of flies 
has also been considered as a scope 2 emission. BT does not generate indirect emissions that need to be considered in this study. 
2.2.1. Exclusions and baseline year 
The direct and indirect GHG sources whose contribution to emissions is not significant (less than 5% of the total emissions) and 
those whose quantification is not technically feasible or cost-effective have been excluded from this study. In this regard, direct GHG 
emissions from the drying kilns were excluded. 
The baseline year inventory can be used to monitor the performance of the process by BT towards certain emission objectives, in 
which case the baseline year is referred to as the target baseline year. The year 2021 has been chosen as the target baseline year. 
2.2.2. Quantification methodology for emissions 
For the present study, the GHG emissions of the company BT have been quantified and documented according to the following 
phases: Identification of emission sources, Selection of quantification methodology, Selection and collection of GHG data from the process, 
Selection or development of GHG emission factors and Calculation of GHG emissions.

The GHG sources of BT contributing to direct GHG emissions (Scope 1), and indirect emissions (Scope 2) have been identified and 
documented separately. The GHG emissions come from the following source categories: Substrate consumption for fly feeding, Electricity

2.4. Water footprint 
To determine the water footprint of BT’s products, the allocation of all farm water consumption is essential. Each final product is 
assigned a share of water based on its selling price. This method ensures a nuanced distribution, attributing a greater responsibility in 
the water footprint to products with higher final values in the sales balance. This meticulous approach aligns with BT’s commitment to 
transparent and comprehensive environmental assessments. 
In the analysis of the water footprint, the sum of green water consumption (rainwater), blue water consumption (water from the 
network), and grey water consumption (water polluted) will be considered. 
The conversion factors for calculating the water footprint have been obtained as follows: the one for electricity was provided by the 
electricity supplier itself (Inicio | Red El´ectrica (ree.es)), and the one for animal feed was determined by considering the conversion 
factors for each cereal it contains, as per specialized literature (Mekonnen and Hoekstra, 2012). This took into account the mass 
percentage of each cereal in the feed. In the case of the water footprint of water consumed on the farm, the supplier’s guidelines 
recommend considering an additional 20% over the actual consumption amount (https://www.emuasa.es/). As for labor, the same 
assumption was made as in calculating the carbon footprint, but with the consideration of emitting 5.5 kg of CO2 for every 40 km 
traveled. To calculate the water footprint of diesel fuel, we used data from Vanham et al., which provides a value of 249 m3 TJ−1 for 
energy generated from petroleum (Vanham et al., 2019). Additionally, we considered information from Hofstrand, who stated that 1 L 
of diesel is equivalent to 3.5 × 10−5 TJ (Hofstrand, 2007). Both the conversion factors for the carbon footprint and the water footprint

3.2. Water footprint 
In the BT company, water consumption originates from two sources: directly within the factory during various production ac­
tivities, including water supplied to feeding trays for larval development, tracked through water billing data as blue water usage. The 
second source involves water indirectly provided to larvae through their diet, predominantly sourced from the water used in cereal 
production. 
As indicated in Table 1, the water directly consumed from the municipal water supply network amounts to 8,467,000 L per year. 
The water indirectly provided can be estimated from the cereals that make up the feed consumed by the flies. These cereals have a 
high content of green water, as they are typically rain-fed crops. In Table 4, the cereals that make up more than 95% of the diet of BT 
farm flies are listed, along with the volume of water consumed in their production. Blue water is from surface and groundwater sources 
used for consumption and irrigation; green water is rainwater absorbed by plants; and grey water is domestic wastewater without fecal 
contamination, reusable after treatment. In Table 4, it is evident that barley, wheat, corn, and beetroot, which constitute the diet of 
Hermetia illucens flies, utilize different volumes of green, blue, and grey water, with barley and wheat being the primary components. 
Drawing insights from the data presented in Table 5, an average water footprint of 1444 cubic meters per ton (m3/t) has been 
employed as the representative measure for the water footprint of the feed. 
The intricate process of allocating the water footprint to each product involves a comprehensive consideration of the overall water 
consumption, encompassing both direct usage and the water embedded in the feed. This allocation is methodically distributed among 
the diverse products manufactured by BT. This intricate procedure unfolds in two distinct phases: initially, products such as eggs, 
larvae, and fertilizer are individually contemplated; subsequently, an in-depth analysis of the larvae’s breakdown into more refined 
products is undertaken. The detailed breakdown of water consumption for each farm input, coupled with their respective percentage 
contributions, is presented in Table 5. This approach ensures a thorough examination of water utilization across various stages of 
production. 
Utilizing the aggregate water consumption data encompassing all activities on the farm, it becomes imperative to allocate a distinct 
water footprint to each product by considering the sales volumes of each product. The rigorous calculation of the water footprint for 
specific products, such as eggs, neonates, fresh larvae, and fertilizer, is presented comprehensively in Table 8 (Appendix). This 
approach ensures a nuanced understanding of the water-related impact associated with each facet of the production of the farm, 
allowing for a granular assessment of resource utilization and environmental responsibility. 
Under the assumption of identical conditions as those stipulated for the carbon footprint analysis, where all fresh larvae are 
commercially sold without deriving any by-products, a water footprint of 1.529 cubic meters of water per kilogram of fresh larvae is 
carefully computed. A comparative examination against the water footprints of conventionally consumed meats like chicken, pork, 
sheep, and beef, registering at 4.325, 5.988, 8.763, and 15.415 cubic meters per ton, respectively (Vauterin et al., 2021), unequivocally 
reveals the markedly reduced water footprint associated with Hermetia illucens larvae produced by BT. This disparity is particularly 
striking in the case of beef, where the water footprint surpasses tenfold. 
It is imperative to note that fresh larvae are not the typical presentation form for BT products intended for consumption. Following 
a drying process that expels 65% of the larva’s mass in the form of water (a percentage analogous to that of traditional farm animals), 
the end result is dried larvae. This form enhances ease of preservation, storage, and transportation.

4. Conclusions 
A comprehensive investigation has been undertaken to analyze the carbon and water footprint of a black soldier fly (Hermetia 
illucens) farm in detailed managed by BioflyTech. Situated in the expansive expanse of 21,000 square meters in the Murcia region of 
Spain, this study adheres to the stringent guidelines outlined by the ISO 14064-1 standard. The examination encompasses a holistic 
approach, delving into both the overall emissions generated by the farm and the emissions intricately associated with each distinct 
activity and product earmarked for commercialization. 
Data pertinent to consumption and production on the farm were diligently collated from diverse sources, with contributions from 
the quality, production, engineering, and finance departments of BioflyTech. These departments, each playing a pivotal role in the 
operations of the fam, have provided essential insights into the intricacies of resource utilization and product generation. The 
collaboration among these sectors ensures a comprehensive and multifaceted understanding of the farm’s environmental impact. 
Furthermore, the emission factors utilized in this study, a critical component for accurate assessment, have been diligently sourced 
from corresponding entities with expertise in emission quantification. These factors, rooted in scientific rigor, enhance the precision of 
the evaluation by incorporating industry-recognized metrics and benchmarks. The collaboration between BioflyTech and these 
external entities underscores the commitment to methodological rigor and accuracy in the determination of both carbon and water 
footprints. 
By embarking on this thorough analysis, BioflyTech endeavors not only to quantify its environmental impact but also to establish a 
benchmark for responsible and sustainable farming practices within the burgeoning sector of black soldier fly cultivation. This study 
aims to contribute valuable insights to the broader discourse on eco-friendly agricultural methodologies, emphasizing the imperative 
of balancing production demands with ecological stewardship. 
The main conclusions of the study are as follows: 
The cumulative emissions from the farm amount to 2,404,691 kg of CO2eq, of which nearly 80% emanate from the production 
process of fly feed. 
The specific operational activity denoted as ‘Feeding Neonate to Larva - Larval Seed - Larva - Adult’ emerges as the predominant 
contributor to the overall carbon footprint within the spectrum of the farm’s activities, constituting more than 50% of the aggregate 
CO2 emissions. 
In the sphere of protein production derived from Hermetia illucens larvae, the emissions are approximately thirty times lower than 
those stemming from cattle (beef) and five times less than those associated with chicken egg production. 
The comprehensive water requirement for the entire spectrum of farm products stands at 7,273,380 cubic meters. 
The water footprint attributed to the production of fresh larval protein is quantified at 1.293 cubic meters per ton, representing a 
markedly lower impact compared to conventional meat proteins commonly consumed. This emphasizes the sustainable advantage of 
larval protein production in mitigating water-related environmental concerns. 
This in-depth analysis not only quantifies the environmental impact of the farm but also underscores the significant role that 
alternative protein sources, such as Hermetia illucens larvae, can play in fostering sustainable agricultural practices. The utilization of 
such data is pivotal for steering agricultural enterprises towards ecologically conscious decisions and contributing to the ongoing 
discourse on sustainable food production.
