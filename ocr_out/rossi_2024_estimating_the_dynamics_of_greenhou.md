A B S T R A C T
Edible insects are considered a sustainable alternative protein source. Several studies have indicated that
greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions from insects are generally low. These data were usually recorded in closed static
chambers, which did not reflect the actual rearing conditions. On the other hand, open respiration chambers
need high amounts of substrate and insects, making the analyses time consuming and expensive. In the present
study, a novel small-scale open gas exchange recording system for evaluation of GHG emissions from insects is
presented. The device consists of four independent respiration chambers, each one equipped with a pump for
supplying air, a flow meter and a septum for sampling the flowing air. A validation experiment was carried out
using black soldier fly larvae as model organisms and moistened chicken feed (dry matter 40%) as rearing
substrate. Three independent runs were conducted, each with two replicates, using 200 neonates larvae and 300
g of substrate. Moistened chicken feed (300 g) without larvae was used as control. Throughout the 12 days
rearing period, gas sampling occurred every 12 h. Data interpolation considering a linear tendency between two
successive sampling points was applied and overall amounts of gases were quantified as area under the curve.
Irrespective of the run, CO2 emissions in the insect-substrate systems increased during the initial 7 d of exper-
iment, consistently followed by a rapid decrease. Multiple cross correlation analysis showed high reproducibility
(r ≥0.9), while significant differences in the CO2 emissions between insects-substrate system (range 7.76–11.88
g g−1 dry insects) and sole substrate (range 0.20–0.33 g g−1 initial dry substrate) were observed. Very limited
N2O (≤1.13 mg g−1 dry insects) and CH4 (≤1.33 mg g−1 dry insects) emissions were detected in this experiment,
indicating that the performed black soldier fly larvae rearing did not result in significant production of these
GHGs. Overall, the experiment showed that the novel device may be particularly suited for quick and reliable
estimation of GHG emissions from edible insects.
1. Introduction
Approximately 30% (15.8 Gt CO2-eq) of the overall (52.5 Gt CO2-eq)
global greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions are generated by the agro-food
system (Li et al., 2022). Excluding the non-food agriculture, the food
supply chain alone is responsible for 13.7 Gt emissions (Poore and
Nemecek, 2018), with 7.4 Gt of CO2-eq originating within the farm gate
(crops and livestock production) (FAO, 2022). Emissions from meat
production are estimated to be approx. 2.8 Gt of CO2-eq per year, ac-
counting for 39% of the overall agriculture GHG emissions (excluding
transport and land use) (Li et al., 2022). A large part of such emissions is
shared among ruminant animals with cattle and sheep responsible for 30
and 24 kg of CO2-eq per kg of meat, respectively (FAO, 2022). Hence, it
is evident that the modern food system is unsustainable and requires
transformation.
Novel and future food sources have been described as perfect means
for ensuring the ecological transition of the food system while safe-
guarding the nutritional quality of the diet (Parodi et al., 2018).
Whether integrated with other novel food sources, inclusion of edible
insects in the daily diet may represent the turning point toward a sus-
tainable food system (Mazac et al., 2022).
Research conducted to date has demonstrated that insects can effi-
ciently grow on waste, by-products and decaying materials while
emitting very low GHG (Moruzzo et al., 2021). Despite insect rearing is

Although such systems can be useful for shaping a first idea on the in-

to offer important information to entrepreneurs and policy makers,

simulating real rearing conditions. This paper presents and describes the
new device in the first part. In the second section, results of a validation
experiment are discussed. Black soldier fly larvae (BSFL) and moistened
chicken feed were used as model objects.
2. Description of the gas emission chamber
The innovative climate-controlled gas exchange system (Fig. 1a) for
continuous recording of insects’ gas emissions was based on a small-
scale respiration chamber for measuring the metabolic activity of fresh
vegetables (Linke et al., 2010; Rux et al., 2017). The new system con-
sisted of four acrylic glass cylinders (gas exchange chamber, V = 9.42 L,
H = 30 cm, Ø = 20 cm, Fig. 1b) in which boxes with substrate and insects
could be housed. Each cylinder could be hermetically sealed by a
removable front acrylic glass push-fit cap (Ø = 22 cm, W = 1 cm) fitted
with polyurethane frame closure connection (2.5 cm height). The clos-
ing system consisted of a rectangular aluminium rod (H = 3 cm, L =
24.5 cm, W = 1 cm) and a polyurethane-screened aluminium cylinder
(Ø = 5.5 cm, W = 1 cm). In each chamber, three sensors recorded
temperature, relative humidity and air pressure ((d), ALMEMO FHAD

rate of each inlet line was independently controlled and regulated by
adjusting the voltage of the pump.
For the outlet system (Fig. 1a, red pipes (e)) 1.0 cm-silicon tubes
(overall length 210 cm) were placed on the front cap of each chamber
and fixed through a 4 cm long polyamide semi-flexible pipe (Landefeld

flowmeter (f), placed on the final section of the outlet tube was finally

In order to ensure exact temperature control, the entire system was
placed inside a climate chamber (Bio Line VB1014, V¨otsch Industri-

conditioned. Besides PC and power supply, flowmeters, inlet sampling
box and outlet tubes including the outlet sampling septa were placed
outside the climate chamber.
3. Materials and methods
3.1. Substrate and insects
Commercial chicken feed (all-mash A Mehl M.Cocc., Deuka com-
panion GmbH, Düsseldorf, Germany) with an initial dry matter content

each, separated by breaks of 30 s to homogenise the bulk. Aliquots

and moistened at approx. 60% of humidity by adding 166.5 g of distilled
water (final substrate mass 300 g). To avoid excess of free water on the
surface, prepared substrates were kept two days (48 ± 2 h) at 10 ◦C
before adding larvae. For each run, four boxes with substrate were

Black soldier fly larvae (BSFL, Hermetia illucens) were chosen as

3.2. Experimental set-up and gas emission measurements
Experiments were repeated three times (three runs). Two indepen-
dent replicates were performed for each run (six replicates in total).
Actual concentration of N2O, CH4 and CO2 emitted by insects and
substrates were discontinuously measured through GC analysis of the air
sampled from the inlet and outlet systems. Gas sampling was carried out
every 12 h (every day at 8:00 a.m. and 8:00 p.m. (± 30 min)), along the
entire insect rearing period of 12 d. At each sampling point, three gas
samples were sampled with a 25 mL gastight glass syringe (1025 TLL,
PTFE Luer Lock, Hamilton Company, Nevada, USA) equipped with a
Sterican Gr2-G21 0.8 mm × 40 mm needle and immediately conveyed in
a pre-evacuate (2.7 mbar of negative pressure) 25 mL glass vial equipped
with a PTFE/butyl rubber septum stopper.
Sampling started approximately 10 h after setting up the box inside
each gas exchange unit (stabilization period = 10 ± 1 h). CO2 and O2
concentrations, relative humidity and pressure inside each unit were
monitored before and after each sampling and the airflow rates were
adjusted to keep the relative humidity inside the unit between 65 and
75% RH.
To minimise the interaction with external environment, gas ex-
change units and climate chamber were not opened during the experi-
ments. After 12 d, all gas exchange chambers were opened and the
rearing boxes were collected. BSFL were harvested from T1 and T2
treatments by handpicking them with forceps, carefully cleaned with
towel paper, counted and weighted on an analytical balance (CPA224S-
0CE, Sartorius AG, G¨ottingen, Germany). Mass of residuals (frass from
T1 and T2, substrate residuals from C1 and C2) was also recorded.
3.3. Gas quantification
Air samples were analysed every Monday, Wednesday and Friday
morning. When needed, air samples were dark-stored in a BD-S 115
safety cabinet (Binder GmbH, Tuttlingen, Germany) at 20 ◦C to minimise
any possible external impact. Air samples were analysed with a GC-2014
gas chromatograph (Shimadzu Deutschland GmbH, Duisburg, Germany)
equipped with FID and ECD detectors as well as a HS-20 auto sampler.
Four different Agilent stainless steel packed columns (Haysep N, 1.0 m;
Haysep D, 4.0 m; Mole Sieve 5A, 2.5 m; Haysep N, 1.5 m) were used
(overall length 9 m, inner diameters 2 mm and mesh size 80/100). Ni-
trogen was used as carrier gas, while hydrogen and synthetic gas
Alphagaz 1 (Air Liquide Deutschland GmbH, Berlin, Germany) were the
fuel gasses for FID.
Air samples (2 mL) from the vials were directly injected into the GC.
Gas flow was set at 25 mL min−1, oven temperature at 80 ◦C, and FID
and ECD temperatures at 250 ◦C and 325 ◦C, respectively. CH4 con-
centration (detection range: 1–100 ppm) was measured directly from
the sample, while CO2 was converted to CH4 within a methaniser
operating at 380 ◦C, prior detection by FID (detection range: 1–100000
ppm). N2O concentration was finally measured by ECD (detection range:
0.05–100 ppm). Effective gas concentration (ppm) was calculated by the
LabSolutions LCGC software (Shimadzu Deutschland GmbH, Duisburg,
Germany) using four standard calibration gas mixtures (Air Liquide
Deutschland GmbH, Düsseldorf, Germany). For each studied gas, intra-
run accuracy of the GC was computed as average of the absolute dif-
ference between the recorded value and the average of three indepen-
dent determinations performed on the same standard mixture.

the measuring device.

4. Results and discussion
The aim of this investigation was to test and validate the new gas
exchange chamber. Three independent runs and two replicates per run
evaluating the GHGs emitted by BSFL reared on moistened chicken feed
were carried out. Therefore, emissions of the system substrate + insects
(treatment T) were compared with the emission of the sole substrate
(treatment C), and experimental reproducibility was evaluated through
a multiple cross correlation analysis.

able (Parodi et al., 2020), the recovered data showed high inter-run
reproducibility, suggesting suitability of the novel device for rapid and

4.1. GHG emissions trends and experimental reproducibility
CO2 emissions from the treatments T (Fig. 2A, red curves) were low
at the beginning, increased after approximately 3.5 d, peaked between
day 5 and day 7 and decreased again up to days 8–9. Such bell-shaped
trend was observed in all the runs, with very good overlapping of
curves. Cross correlation analysis (supplementary material S1) dis-
played a strong significant positive intra and inter run correlation (r ≥
0.9) between treatments T, which suggested high reproducibility of the
process. The aforementioned dynamics have already been noticed by
other authors (Bekker et al., 2021; Ermolaev et al., 2019; Pang et al.,
2020a; Perednia et al., 2017), further supporting the validity of the
measuring procedure. The main explanation for this particular dynamic
might be found in the activity of BSFL and in the nature of the rearing
substrate. CO2 is mainly originated from microbial and larval meta-
bolism (Boakye-Yiadom et al., 2022) whereby it is plausible to expect
low emissions at the beginning, followed by a fast increase and a final
reduction and stabilization. Bekker et al. (2021) also observed that with
a substrate moisture of 65–75%, larvae started producing significant
CO2 only after reaching 5–6 mg of mass. After this point, linear corre-
lation between CO2 production and larval mass was usually observed
(Laganaro et al., 2021). In the present experiment, larvae less than 12 h
old were used. Consequently, they could have had a very low activity
during the first hours of experiment, explaining the low CO2 emission
observed at the beginning. As the larvae grew, their metabolic activity (i.
e., respirational activity) increased (Gligorescu et al., 2019), resulting in
higher CO2 release. Similarly, the reduction of CO2 release observed at
the end of each run could suggest a decline in larvae metabolic activity,
mainly due to the achievement of the critical mass (i.e., the minimum
mass to allow metamorphosis) as well as to a substantial reduction of the
readily available feed (Bekker et al., 2021; Gligorescu et al., 2019).
Chemical analyses of substrate before and after (frass) the BSFL growth
are shown in supplementary material S2. Considering the different dry
matter recorded for substrate and frass, a reduction of approximately
50% of total volatile solids was detected. It was accompanied by a sig-
nificant decline in TOC (47–53%) and NDF (15–24%) contents, while
absolute amounts of ADF and ADL increased. Different from NDF, which
comprises cellulose, hemicellulose and lignin (Hindrichsen et al., 2006),
ADF and ADL represents the amount of only hemicellulose and lignin
(Peguero et al., 2023). While cellulose is known to be easily degraded by
BSFL, hemicellulose and lignin are not directly digested (Gold et al.,
2018), pointing out that after 12 d of BSFL composting, no easily
digestible substrate was available. On the other hand, although the in-
sects might have had already reached a satisfactory weight for going
through the metamorphosis, they still appeared far from the pupal stage,
suggesting that provision of new fresh feed (re-feeding) could have
reactivated the metabolism, resulting in higher final larval weight and a
new CO2 emission peak (Zhang et al., 2021).
CO2 emissions from the sole substrates (Fig. 2A, green curves,
treatments C) were roughly stable during the first week of the experi-
ment and increased slightly only after this period. In these substrates,
CO2 emissions could mainly be associated to microbial activity (Oonincx
et al., 2010) as high fungal growth was observed in every run. Microbial
activity is well known for playing a crucial role in substrate modification
and CO2 emissions (Ermolaev et al., 2019). Future studies should focus
on understanding the effect of microorganisms on the GHG emissions of
substrate with and without BSFL.
Concerning CH4 (Fig. 2B) and N2O (Fig. 2C), no clear dynamics were
observed for either treatments T or C. The absence of consistency on
such data was also confirmed by the cross correlation, which highlighted
no correlation at the inter-run level, although some positive correlations
were observed within the same run (supplementary material S1). One
sample-t-test (supplementary material S3) and comparisons between
effective gas concentration and accuracy (supplementary material S4) of
the measuring device showed that no difference from zero were statis-
tically observed, suggesting that BSFL did not emit any CH4 or N2O in
our experiment. It is consistent with previous reports, which highlighted
very low or absence of production of such GHGs by BSFL (Boakye-Yia-
dom et al., 2022; Perednia et al., 2017), which is strictly dependent on
the physico-chemical characteristics and nature of the rearing substrate
(Chen et al., 2019; Ermolaev et al., 2019; Sandrock et al., 2019; Pang
et al., 2020a; Mertenat et al., 2019).
4.2. Cumulative gas emissions and mass balance
Cumulative gas emissions highlighted that BSFL were mainly
responsible for producing CO2, while no significant emissions of CH4
and N2O could be detected (Table 1). It is in accordance with previous
studies that suggested very limited CH4 and N2O emissions, compared
with direct CO2 emissions (Lindberg et al., 2022).
Two-sample t-test carried out on the overall dataset detected sig-
nificant difference between the system substrate + insects and the sole
substrate only for CO2 (P = 0.001), suggesting that BSFL were the main
contributors for the CO2 emissions of the system. An increase in CO2
emissions due to BSFL activity was also observed in the BSFL-mediated
composting process of manure when compared to classical composting
(Parodi et al., 2021). On the other hand, no statistically significant dif-
ferences between treatments T and C were computed for CH4 (P =
0.406) and N2O (P = 0.428) cumulative emissions, confirming that
production of such gasses was mainly due to microbial activity on the
substrate, as already stated by Oonincx et al. (2010) and Parodi et al.
(2021). However, although significant differences in CO2 emissions
were detected across the entire experiment, the intra-run ANOVA
showed significant differences between treatment T and treatment C
only in RUN-2 (P = 0.002). This highlighted that other parameters, such
as environmental conditions, feeding status and larval metabolic activity
might have influenced the overall GHG emissions from insects. Possible
explanation for the no statistical significance in runs 1 and 3 could
indeed be the low number of larvae compared with the high amount of
substrate used in the present study as well as the already mentioned high
microbial activity. The later might also be the main explanation for the
higher amount of N2O recorded from the treatment T in RUN-1,
compared to the other runs. Furthermore, it should be noted that,
although not important differences were observed on the substrate
composition between different runs (supplementary material S2), the
insects reared on RUN-1 showed lower growth and slightly higher sur-
vival (Table 2). These two aspects together might have affected the
substrate turning activity, resulting in different air circulation within the
bulk and promoting the selection of specific microbial species, particu-
larly active on denitrification and N2O production, as already observed
by Nigussie et al. (2016) in heartworm-mediated composting processes.
Chemical analyses of frass (supplementary material S2) originated along
the three runs support such statement. Although an increase in nitrate
and nitrite from substrate to frass was observed in all the run, the
amounts of both nitrate and nitrite measured in frass from the two
treatments in RUN-1 were significantly lower than those measured in
the frass from the other two runs. Since N2O is primarily originates from
nitrate and nitrite reduction (Thamdrup, 2012), lower concentration of
these compounds in frass may suggest higher denitrification. On the
other hand, it is also interesting to observe that the amount of nitrite in
residuals of treatments C from RUN-1 was significantly higher than the
amount of nitrite in residuals generated in runs 2 and 3. This suggests
that the denitrification process and overall N2O production are more
influenced by general environmental conditions rather than by the
presence and activity of BSFL. However, it should be mentioned that
such phenomena may not be universally applicable as higher larval
density and/or lower feeding rate could also impact the microenviron-
ment inside the substrate (Chen et al., 2019).
CO2 cumulative emissions computed as g of gas per kg of dry insects,
ranged between 11880 and 7760 (Table 1). Despite the high variability

observed between the three runs, which may be explained by the un-
controlled microbial contribution, as well as by different insects’ per-
formances, cumulative CO2 production was always significantly higher
than in other studies (Boakye-Yiadom et al., 2022). Several aspects
including the frequency of gas measurements, the number of reared
larvae, the rearing substrate and the abiotic conditions should be
considered to explain such difference. For example, Parodi et al. (2020)
observed that increasing the gas sampling frequency resulted in higher
cumulative CO2 detection, thereby decreasing the evaluation errors.
Additionally, Pang et al. (2020b) and Chen et al. (2019) showed that
CO2 emissions were positively correlated with substrate C/N ratio and
moisture, respectively, suggesting that the substrate plays an important
role in determining the actual GHG emissions. Additionally, it should be
mentioned that 12 h old larvae were used in the present study, while
other studies usually used 3 d, 5 d, 7 d or 10 d old larvae (Boakye-Yia-
dom et al., 2022). As young larvae are more active in terms of metabolic
performances (Gligorescu et al., 2019), it could be hypothesised that
neonate larvae produced more CO2 than older larvae. This justifies the
higher emission detected in the present study and suggests that emis-
sions from BSFL in other studies are probably underestimated. In the
present study, CO2 cumulative emissions computed per mass unit of dry
insects did not seem to be strongly related to the larval growth perfor-
mances, although good correlation was computed between larval gained
mass and CO2 emission (Spearman’s rank correlation: ρ = 0.89, P =
0.0333). Low correlation was instead detected between substrate
reduction and CO2 emission (Spearman’s rank correlation: ρ = 0.26, P =
0.6583), as well as between CO2 emission and FCR (Spearman’s rank
correlation: ρ = −0.60, P = 0.2417). It confirms the findings of Chen
et al. (2019), Eriksen (2022) and Laganaro et al. (2021), while contra-
dicts Oonincx et al. (2010) and Lindberg et al. (2022), who observed an
inverse correlation between FCR and CO2 production and a direct
correlation between substrate reduction and CO2 production.
CH4 emissions varied between 760 and 1328 mg kg−1 of dry BSFL
(Table 1). Such values were within the range of published data (between
5.5 and 10066 mg kg−1 of dry BSFL) (Boakye-Yiadom et al., 2022), and
confirms that methane production relies more on the rearing substrate
than on the BSFL. Indeed, high CH4 emission is only observed when
manure or decaying material is used (Parodi et al., 2021), while it is
negligible when chicken feed is the rearing substrate (Perednia et al.,
2017). However, it should be noted that our values are higher than
already published values on commercial feed (Parodi et al., 2020). Such
difference might be explained by the different experimental set-up
applied in our study. Specifically, while Parodi et al. (2020) reared
30000 larvae on 12 kg of substrate, a very limited larvae/substrate ratio
(200 larvae on 300 g of feed) was applied in our study. In such condi-
tions, we might hypothesise that anoxic areas were generated inside the
substrate, favouring the growth of methanogenic bacteria, which may
have produced CH4 (Chen et al., 2019). Similar considerations could
also be valid for N2O. Emissions of this GHG indeed usually range be-
tween 0.33 and 118 mg kg−1 of dry larvae (Boakye-Yiadom et al., 2022).
Significantly higher N2O emissions were recorded in our experiment,
ranging between 275 and 1130 mg kg−1 of dry larvae (Table 1). The
higher N2O emissions measured in the present study might be explained
by the high feeding rate (i.e. 0.6 g of dry substrate/larvae or 50 mg of dry
feed/larvae/day) as higher feeding rate has been associated to higher N
emissions (Nigussie et al., 2017; Rossi et al., 2023). The high variability
observed in the present experiment was mainly due to the RUN-1.
Indeed, as already mentioned, smaller larvae were collected in such
treatments. The reduced mobility of smaller larvae within the substrate
could lead to lower oxygen dissolution and higher denitrification
(Thamdrup et al., 2012), which is also supported by the lower concen-
tration of nitrate and nitrite in the frass (supplementary information 2).
Despite the differences in cumulative gas emitted across the three
runs, the mass balance of C and N in substrate + insects treatments
displayed an overall recovery of approximately 93% and 85–95%,
respectively (Figs. 3a and 4a). Similar recovery was also observed for C
in the sole substrate treatments (92–93%; Fig. 3b), while higher vari-
ability was detected for N (80–99%; Fig. 4b). Although such values are
still far from 100%, based on the observations of Parodi et al. (2020) and
Pang et al. (2020a) they can still be considered satisfactory. Specifically,
Parodi et al. (2020) observed an overall recovery rate of 95% for C and
101% for N by using a continuous air detection system, while Pang et al.
(2020a) were able to recover 87.48–97.90% of the initial C and
74.40–98.39% of the initial N, when BSFL were reared on food waste.
In the present study, the mentioned recovery rates were similarly to
those of these studies (Pang et al., 2020a; Parodi et al., 2020) and the
variability on gas emission quantification was lower than in Mertenat
et al. (2019). This indicates that the novel gas emission chamber works
and presents high capability for large-scale implementation. However,

Although NH3 is not a direct GHG, it is often considered as main
responsible for water and soil pollution (IPCC, 2014). Additionally, it
might be oxidised to N2O (FAO, 2006; Iwasa et al., 2015) contributing to
increase its level in air. As NH3 production is usually favoured by high
temperature, elevated pH and increased substrate aeration (Pang et al.,
2020a; Zhang et al., 2021), it can be hypothesised that significant pro-
duction of such gas occurred, especially during the first days of con-
version (Chen et al., 2019). It was further suggested by the strong acrid
smell perceived at the end of the outlet system as well as by the signif-
icant increase of NH4–N concentration observed in both, T and C
treatments (supplementary material S2). NH4–N may originate from
protein degradation and mineralisation, as well as from nitrite reduction
(Alvarez et al., 2014; Kroupina et al., 2022). While nitrite reduction is
hardly conceivable in the studied conditions, mineralisation of protein
can be hypothesised. Considering that NH4–N production is known to be
responsible for increased NH3 emissions (Yan et al., 2018; Liu et al.,
2019), it is reasonable to conclude that significant NH3 production
occurred in the present experiment.
Different NH3 production could be the reason for the high variation
in the total N recovery of all treatments. Production of this gas is
extremely variable and depending on several factors, such as aeration
rate, moisture, temperature and pH (Groot Koerkamp, 1994). Although
detection of these factors was continuously carried out during the three
experimental runs, no significant variations were observed (supple-
mentary material S5) between the runs, excluding a potential effect of
the environment on the overall NH3 emissions. On the other hand, it has
been hypothesised that different BSFL populations have different ability
on producing NH3 with not all the individuals being able to do it (Parodi
et al., 2020). Another explanation could be that NH3 emissions from
BSFL rearing were strongly impacted by the larval metabolic perfor-
mances (Parodi et al., 2021) as well as by the substrate microbial activity
(Gold et al., 2018). Specifically, as BSFL grow, significant amounts of
uric acid are produced, which are typically converted into NH3 by mi-
croorganisms (Green and Popa, 2012).
High variability on percentage of N recovery was also observed in the
treatment C (Fig. 4b) indicating different intensities of nitrification
processes in the substrates. The analysed different contents of NO2-,
NO3- and NH4–N in the substrates (supplementary material 2) indicate
the volatilisation of a significant amount of NH4–N as NH3 (Ren et al.,
2018).

5. Conclusions
The present study showed that the novel gas emission chamber with
continuous airflow is well suited for fast and reliable estimations of GHG
emissions from edible insects. Although it was a first trial using the new
device, gases emitted by BSFL grown on chicken feed were detected with
high reproducibility in terms of CO2 emission rate. However, the same
level of reproducibility was not observed for CH4 and N2O emissions,
which were generally limited and substrate dependent. Cumulative
emissions computed as g g−1 (gas/dry insect) were significantly higher
than previously reported values, likely due to the use of younger larvae
in this experiment. However, the absence of a continuous measurement
method and the assumption that the airflow was linear between two
successive measuring points might have led to overestimation of the
emissions. Acceptable overall C recovery was detected for all runs, with
values not significantly different from the ones reported in literature.
Rather low N recovery percentage was instead detected as no NH3
measurement was implemented.
Future studies should aim to improve the device by increasing the
number of chambers in order to increase the number of parallel repe-
titions, implementing high-accuracy continuous emission detection
systems and developing a NH3 quantification method. Other substrates
such as waste and manure, where CH4 and N2O emissions have already
been detected, should also be tested with the aim of validate the device
for gases other than CO2. Moreover, since the system showed low feed
conversion efficiency and significant energy loss, further improvements
on the experimental design, such as reduction of insects to substrate
ratio, longer rearing period or repeated feeding events should be
considered.
