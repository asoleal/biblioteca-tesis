A B S T R A C T  
Composting has been used extensively as a sustainable approach for the management organic waste streams and 
conversion into stabilized soil amendments and horticultural growth media. In addition to these products, the 
composting process also generates by-products of microbial metabolism that includes gas (CO2) and energy 
(heat). High rates of food waste and commercial organic waste generation, along with greater diversion away 
from landfills, creates new opportunities to valorize these by-products from aerobic composting. Specifically, 
integrating carbon dioxide recapture and recycling into Controlled Environment Agriculture (CEA) production 
systems presents a pathway to circularize resources. CEA systems produce plants year round in an environ­
mentally controlled space where elevating CO2 environments can boost plant production, creating a synergistic 
relationship with composting processes. The review evaluated CO2 emissions from an array of open and closed 
composting systems, as well as different feedstocks to demonstrate its potential for use in CEA. Rates of CO2 
emissions varied widely, with a peak CO2 emission range of 0.32–429.27 g CO2–C kg−1 d−1 depending on the 
aeration conditions and feedstock composition. Analysis of two hypothetical CEA scenarios reveals that inte­
grating in-vessel composting as an on-site waste management process is comparable in cost to using natural gas 
as a regular CO2 source. The quantity of composting feedstocks required to generate CO2 emissions for a single 
tier and five tier CEA lettuce production system ranged from 3.38 to 69.94 kg dry biomass d−1 and 9.93–205.50 
kg dry biomass d−1, respectively, but varied based on feedstock composition and compost operating conditions. 
The long-term research goal is toward valorizing non-edible biomass of CEA systems to create a circular economy 
model of commercial plant production.   
1. Introduction 
Rapid urbanization aligned with global population growth results in 
significant quantities of organic wastes being generated annually (Bong 
et al., 2017; Karak et al., 2012; Narayana, 2009). These include food 
wastes from residential, commercial, and manufacturing sectors (Meli­
koglu et al., 2013; Julian et al., 2010) as well as secondary food pro­
cessing by-products from industry or municipal wastewater solids 
(Hackett et al., 1999; Villase˜nor et al., 2011). Traditional methods of 
organic waste management and disposal, such as landfilling (Louis, 
2004) and incineration (Assamoi and Lawryshyn, 2012; Cherubini et al., 
2009) are unsustainable and a transition toward prevention, recovery, 
diversion, and valorization of organic matter, as outlined by the food 
waste/recovery hierarchy (US EPA, 2015a) has occurred. For instance, 
modern landfill designs not only incorporate leachate collection and 
treatment, but also methane capture as a bioenergy source,(Adhikari 
et al., 2006; Behera et al., 2010; Themelis and Ulloa, 2007). Many waste 
management authorities around the world are moving to ban landfilling 
of organic wastes (Behera et al., 2010; BioCycle, 2018; California 
Legislature, 2014; Government of Nova Scotia, 1994; Government of 
Ontario, 2017). In Canada, some jurisdictions have either banned the 
landfilling of organic waste (Nova Scotia and Prince Edward Island) or 
are in transitionary phases (Ontario) to do so (Government of Ontario, 
2017). Despite this progress, food wastes (edible and non-edible) 
contribute 23%, or 6 million tonnes, of the total Canadian municipal 
solid waste (MSW) stream sent to landfill (Environment and Climate 
Change Canada, 2020). Organics recycling programs are now aimed at 
diversion and conversion into new products or developing energy

Furthermore, municipal and commercial sectors are valorizing organic

Organic waste generation is increasing at unprecedented rates due to 
rapid urbanization and economic development globally (Muller, 2009). 
Globally, from 2007 to 2017, the top five main crops (sugar cane, maize, 
wheat, rice and potatoes) have seen an increase in production both on 
account of food demand and for biofuel production, especially with 
maize (FAO, 2019). Maize production increased from 792 million tonnes 
in 2007 to 1.13 billion tonnes in 2017, while the other main commod­
ities also experienced sizable increases: 606 to 771 million tonnes 
(wheat), 656 to 769 million tonnes (rice) and 314 to 388 million tonnes 
(potatoes). Similar production trends can be seen in meat, dairy, and egg 
products, with chicken numbers increasing by 4.5 billion head in 2007 
to 22.8 billion in 2017. Similarly an increase in fish production has gone 
from 98 million tonnes in 1990 to 173 million tonnes in 2017, pre­
dominantly driven by the expansion of aquaculture (FAO, 2019). Global 
food loss and food waste (FLW) ranging from unavoidable food pro­
duction losses to avoidable waste,(including discards and unpurchased 
material) is estimated to be 1.3 billion tonnes according to 2019 FAO 
statistics, and within North America, the USA generates an estimated 
168 million tonnes of FLW (FAO, 2019), while Canadian FLW is esti­
mated to be 34.9million tonnes (Gooch et al., 2019). 
In agriculture, animal manures and slurries have an intrinsic value to 
the agricultural community, providing a source of energy and nutrients 
in the form of nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and micro-nutrients for 
crop production. Similarly, food wastes have value past their original 
purpose of human (or animal) consumption, as a source of energy and 
nutrients in a range of valorization technologies. Anaerobic digestion 
(AD) is one favoured approach for treating food wastes, using biogas as a 
waste-to-energy fuel, with the resultant digestate land-applied for crop 
production. The European anaerobic digestion market is projected to be 
worth CAD$94.2 billion by 2026 (Global Market Insights Inc, 2020), 
demonstrating the tangible economic value of recycling organic waste 
materials. Anaerobic digestion can be a valuable and important method 
of utilizing waste material, already an important component of the Eu­
ropean energy mix (International Renewable Energy Agency, 2018). A 
significant disadvantage of AD technologies is the large capital-intensive 
infrastructure and government-subsidized electrical supply rates, as well 
as a local land-base for disposal of the digestate. In North America, the 
cost of infrastructure and jurisdictional variability on subsidizing or 
supporting electrical generation from alternative energy sources are still 
barriers for wide-scale adoption of AD(Canadian Biogas Association, 
2019). Other routes for organic food wastes include rendering of animal 
by-products (for protein meal and oil products), autoclaving and hy­
drolysis, incineration (or waste-to-energy), pyrolysis, gasification, and 
plasma gasification. Each of these methodologies have benefits and can 
produce valuable products, but are also associated with constraints on 
the raw material composition, are capital-intensive to build and operate, 
and at best are more suited to large-scale industrial settings where vol­
umes of waste material generated are high, land is expensive, and energy 
recovery is viable(NI Business Info, 2012). 
Composting is a viable process to optimally manage the biological 
decomposition of organic matter under aerobic conditions. As an aerobic 
decomposition process, the resulting predominant products include 
stabilized organic matter (compost), water, heat, water vapour, and 
carbon dioxide (Diaz and de Bertoldi, 2007; Epstein, 2017). The quantity 
of CO2, and of other gases (N2O and CH4), produced will be influenced 
by a number of factors including aeration rate, mixing rate, feedstock 
composition, C:N ratio (Jiang et al., 2011), moisture content (El Kader 
et al., 2007), and the temperature profile during the process. Aerobic 
composting is achieved through a number of different processes, ranging 
from outdoor windrows or static piles to indoor in-vessel systems (US 
EPA, 2015b). Han et al. (2018) evaluated the influence of ambient 
temperatures on greenhouse gas generation from sewage sludge pro­
cessed in a large-scale municipal composting facility. Evaluated over 
three seasons (winter, spring and summer), Han et al. found clear 
variation in CO2 emissions between the three different seasons, finding 
higher totalCO2 emissions in spring followed by summer, and winter, 
941.72 mg CO2 kg−1 h−1, 698.97 mg CO2 kg−1 h−1 and 220.54 mg CO2 
kg−1 h−1, respectively. Compost has an established commercial and 
agricultural value as a soil amendment, nursery growth media, and as an 
approach to deal with organic wastes across a range of management 
scales (Hubbe et al., 2010; Meyer-Kohlstock et al., 2013; Walker et al., 
2006). In the USA, composting comprises approximately 8.5% of MSW 
(292.4 million tonnes) management processes(US EPA, 2017) and is 
considered a core process, along with other technologies such as 
anaerobic digestion and incineration(Ayilara et al., 2020; US EPA, 2017; 
Razza et al., 2018). Municipal composting is challenging due to the 
heterogeneity of material, high requirement for labour, time, and cost to 
separate contaminants such as plastics, glass, and other materials, but 
also due to the difficulty in controlling the source material. The final 
product varies in quality and consistency between jurisdictions leading 
to a price per tonne of compost ranging from CAD$30 to $130. 
Increasingly, the economics of municipal organics diversion programs 
face significant challenges ranging from high labour, infrastructure, and 
transportation costs, to low market value for composted products. 
Pandyaswargo and Dickella (2014) evaluated the economics of munic­
ipal composting in Asian cities, determining that factors such as: loca­
tion, scale, processing methods, technology used, end-product quality, 
tipping fees, government incentives or rebates, and the ability to find a 
suitable market for the end-product drive financial feasibility. Coker 
(2010) found that controlling costs and maximising processing capac­
ities is fundamental in ensuring profitability of municipal composting 
facilities (Coker, 2010), while the NorthEast Recycling Council stated 
that 87% of US composting operations fail within five years without 
government subsidies (Castagnero, 2014). Development of circular 
economies, with emphasis on improved valorization of bio-waste 
streams, can generate new opportunities to reduce operational costs, 
improve efficiency of utilization, and build synergies with different 
sectors of the economy (Masullo, 2017). 
Composting of organics is a vital step in reducing greenhouse gas 
emissions, reducing the impact of organics going to landfill and pushing 
forward the concept of a circular economy where little to no material is 
deemed valueless (Favoino and Hogg, 2008; Lou and Nair, 2009). The 
economics of commercial composting systems comprises: 1) the initial 
capital investment for infrastructure and machinery costs, and, 2) 
ongoing expenses including labour, maintenance, utilities and local and 
provincial waste disposal rates. Revenues are generated through gate 
(tipping) fees and the sale of compost. Composting has become not only 
more profitable due to the use of gate fees, but due to higher gate fees 
associated with landfilling. The 2018 median gate fee for landfilling 
within the UK was found to be CAD$182 tonne−1 compared to CAD$78 
tonne−1 for in-vessel composting and CAD$40 for windrow composting 
(WRAP, 2019). Murphy and Power (2006) evaluated four municipal 
composting systems in Ireland, finding that the greater the size of the 
system, the more economically viable they became. Profits generated 
were shown to be predominantly from gate fees, although the sale of 
compost also provided moderate contributions to revenues from these 
systems (Murphy and Power, 2006). Similarly, Pandyaswargo and Pre­
makumara (2014) evaluated the financial sustainability of six com­
posting facilities in developing Asian countries, finding that medium and 
medium-large facilities were the most viable, concluding that gate fees 
and the sale of compost were important in making the facilities 
economically viable; however, the selection of composting technology, 
management practices, market access, and availability of carbon credits 
were also key. Organic matter, from pre- and post-consumer food 
wastes, animal and agricultural wastes, and municipal wastewater solids 
have intrinsic value as sources of energy (carbon) and nutrients (Pham

Rapid expansion of controlled environment agriculture (CEA) in 
recent years provides a new pathway toward food security and resource 
recovery (Hagbert and Bradley, 2017). Innovative practices for 
year-round CEA, as exemplified in the proliferation of vertical farming 
systems, has clear potential to valorize and use residual organic biomass 
from these systems toward improving production efficiencies and 
reducing operational costs. Vertical farming was valued at CAD$2.89 
billion in 2018 and is estimated to reach CAD$16.05 billion by 2026 
(Patil and Baul, 2019). Controlled environment agriculture systems, 
such as vertical farms, are energy and capital intensive, integrating 
multiple components such as, HVAC for environmental control of tem­
perature, humidity, and ambient gas composition, and electric lighting 
systems. Plant growth is modulated through the use of targeted spectral 
compositions using energy efficient LED lights and in many facilities 
through carbon dioxide gas enrichment of the growing environment to 
increase baseline productivity. These systems provide the benefit of 
year-round production, including some reduction of the carbon footprint 
associated with long distance transportation, but comes at the expense 
of higher energy usage and cost. In contrast, large traditional green­
house operations, including some with hydroponic systems, are 
increasingly using technologies found in vertical farming systems to 
improve production, including LED lighting systems (supplemental), 
solar capture, water capture and reuse, and general improvements in 
controlled environment systems (Kalantari et al., 2017). Integrating 
technological advances in recapture and utilization of CO2 from com­
posting of organic wastes can minimize the carbon footprint of CEA 
operations and improve plant productivity rates. The vertical farming 
sector (both large and small operations) is estimated to reachCAD$5.91 
billion globally in 2021, and projected to reach CAD$26.12 billion by 
2028 (Grand View Research, 2021). Vertical Farm systems are touted as 
being more water efficient (Avgoustaki and Xydis, 2020) and productive 
than conventional agriculture, while also reducing the carbon footprint 
associated with food transportation(Al-Kodmany, 2018; de Oliveira 
et al., 2020). Inherent advantages of vertical growing systems, and more 
broadly CEA in general, are the capacity to recycle nutrients more effi­
ciently, improved water use efficiency through capture and reuse of 
irrigation/fertigation solutions, and reduced the use of pesticides. The 
advantage of locally grown foods includes a reduced transportation 
carbon footprint, and fresher products that are closer to market, and 
thereby typically have longer [consumer] shelf life. Further, there is also 
more direct support of local economies when production is based within 
a given community. Global production of lettuce ranges from 26 to 29 
million tonnes over an area of 1,227,358 ha, with an estimated 
field-based production between 2.2 and 3.2 kg lettuce m−2 yr−1 (Naus, 
2018; Shatilov et al., 2019). In contrast, VF systems are estimated to 
generate 41 kg lettuce m−2 yr−1, which is 13–18.5 times more produc­
tion per square metre (Barbosa et al., 2015; Benke and Tomkins, 2017). 
However, if lettuce root biomass is approximately 10–20% of total fresh 
biomass under aeroponic or hydroponic production, which would 
equate to 4.1–8.2 kg root biomass m−2 yr−1, waste biomass generated in 
VF systems under current annual yields would be 2.9 to 5.8 million 
tonnes per year(Li et al., 2018). Under conventional agriculture, the root 
biomass would remain in the soil, recycling the carbon back into the 
system. However, in CEA or VF, current industry practices for residual 
biomass include disposal at local waste facilities or landfills and offsite 
composting where available. The cost of landfilling or recycling will 
vary based on local gate fees, accessibility to centralized organics 
composting facilities, cropping cycle, local by-laws, and the size of the 
vertical farm. Disposal costs for CEA or VF facilities will depend also on 
crop type, the amount of non-commercial residual biomass generated, (i. 
e., root mass, stalk/stems/leaves) and the ratio of harvestable to 
non-edible biomass (harvest index).Another significant implicit chal­
lenge in these production systems is the use of fossil fuels or 
carbon-negative sources to enrich the growing environments with CO2 
(Esmeijer and Bloem, 1999; Ghiat et al., 2021). The overall cost of CO2 is 
not necessarily a major factor (in the absence of a carbon tax) but is 
generated through consumption of fossil fuels(Ghiat et al., 2021; Poudel 
and Dunn, 2017). Atmospheric enrichment of the production facility 
beyond ambient CO2 can dramatically increase production and improve 
profitability. 
On-site processing of waste biomass in CEA or VF systems using in- 
vessel composting may have value by reducing third-party collection, 
transport, and gate fee costs. Moreover, the potential exists for inte­
gration of in-vessel composting to utilize metabolic carbon dioxide in 
plant growth rooms and to generate final compost media for use as a 
nutrient source or seed growth media. With 592 cultivation licences 
granted in Canada, the recent rapid growth of cannabis production fa­
cilities across Canada opens new opportunities for the recycling of waste 
biomass into gas and heat by-products that can be utilized within the 
closed growing environments (Health Canada, 2018). Many CEA 
cannabis operations incorporate CO2 enrichment of the production 
chambers using non-renewable resources. The capacity to integrate 
these two types of technologies currently exists but economies of scale 
related to soil-based production agriculture have been an economic 
bottleneck. Currently, shifting public perception of the environmental 
impacts from soil-based agriculture, increased demand for high value 
fresh produce throughout the year, and the vulnerabilities in global food 
supply chains exposed by the Covid-19 pandemic are opening up the 
opportunities to capitalize on this technology integration (Market Line, 
2020)(Storey and Heliospectra, 2021). 
The scientific literature on composting focuses primarily on chemical 
dynamics of open-air/open access composting systems and/or on the 
generation of compost products (Reyes-Torres et al., 2018). A significant 
gap exists within this body of scientific literature on how the composting 
process and available technologies can be adapted to recapture CO2 gas 
for utilization in other systems, such as vertical farms or CEA. This re­
view aims to highlight the potential to valorize by-product gas, specif­
ically carbon dioxide (CO2) from the composting of organic wastes from 
municipal, commercial, or agricultural sources for use in vertical 
farming and other controlled environment agriculture production 
systems. 
2. Methodology 
To achieve a comprehensive review and analysis of data on CO2 
emissions from composting, a systematic evaluation of the scientific 
literature and government publications was undertaken (Fig. 1). Scien­
tific databases used to identify suitable literature for inclusion in this 
study were accessed and included: Google Scholar (http://scholar. 
google.ca), Science Direct (http://sciencedirect.com), SpringerLink 
(http://springerlink.com) and Web of Science (http://webofknowledge. 
com). 
Utilizing database search functions, key search terms were input 
based upon defined parameters: 1) the main metric relating to CO2, 2) 
the process of CO2 generation, 3) materials used in compost production, 
and 4) the composting systems being evaluated. The search parameters 
were broken in to three stages, with incremental specificity at each stage 
(Fig. 1). 
Each database provided a numerical value of results based on the 
search parameters. Based upon the number of results, additional 
filtering was applied to narrow the search results and remove articles 
with materials not deemed to be as relevant. For example, an overlap 
exists in the application of composts to soils with the use of biochar; 
however, since the focus is on process generated CO2 rather than carbon 
sequestration, journal articles where biochar was part of the composting

2.1. Data reporting 
A challenge in reviewing and synthesizing the literature on CO2 
emissions from composting is the large variation in how data are 
measured, and units presented across studies. A number of different 
analytical techniques and instrumentation are available for the capture 
and measurement of CO2 gas, including gas chromatography, portable 
gas analysers (IRGA), NSDAIR sensors, and alkali traps and back- 
titration using acids. While the various approaches can provide com­
parable results, there may be variations in the sensitivities and in the 
format that data are presented. The data evaluated in this review orig­
inate from studies conducted under open and isolated conditions, which 
affect the extrapolation of gas production in real-world settings. This 
review classifies open systems as any system that is open to the atmo­
sphere such as, windrows, aerated piles, and static piles. Closed or iso­
lated systems include material enclosed within a vessel either at a 
commercial, pilot, or lab/bench-top scale. 
A commonality in reporting units of CO2 is important for comparing 
different data sets. For instance, many studies expressed respiration in 
terms of cumulative emissions (g CO2 kg), daily (g CO2 kg−1 d−1), or 
hourly, (g CO2 kg−1h−1) emissions. Where data was not presented in 
these units, values were converted into a standard format provided other 
data were available in the manuscript, specifically the weight of the 
material being composted (wet weight or dry weight) and the duration 
of each study. This review presents other units, grouped together, when 
conversion was not possible. In some studies, data has been reported on 
an area or flux basis, i.e. g CO2 m−2 d−1 or g CO2 m, and were used when 
the results could be directly compared to other research. In some cases, 
gas units were presented without supporting data to allow for conver­
sion and comparison, g CO2 or g CO2 d−1, but were used when general 
trends could be inferred. This review further sought information relating 
to peak CO2 emissions; data representation is based upon the point in the 
composting process where the maximum CO2 emissions occurred and 
linked to whether the system was open or closed. Differentiation has 
been made between peak CO2 emissions from open and closed systems.

of microbial organisms, with bacterial species typically dominating,

Firmicutes, and Sordariomycetes (Huhe et al., 2017). Microbial groups

enzymes released onto surfaces or in the pore solution (Huhe et al., 
2017). A proportion of the organic carbon is released as carbon dioxide 
during the process of oxidative decomposition, typically 50–60% of the 
total carbon is lost over time(Chang et al., 2019). The rate and quantity 
of carbon loss during organic matter degradation in compost systems 
varies as a function of management practices (Tiquia et al., 2002) 
employed to ensure adequate levels of available oxygen, unsaturated 
moisture conditions, and balancing the C:N ratio of the mixture(USDA - 
Natural Resources Conservation Service, 2007).The consumption of O2 
and the production of CO2 is a clear indicator of the aerobic processes 
working within the compost system, while CH4 is an indicator of 
anaerobic processes and N2O and indication of nitrification(USDA - 
Natural Resources Conservation Service, 2000). 
Composting can be divided into three phases of microbial activity: 1) 
active, during which labile carbon is rapidly mineralized intoCO2 or 
immobilized back to organic forms, 2) stabilisation, during which the 
remaining carbon compounds are either more recalcitrant to degrada­
tion or more complex forms of carbon are created that are also resistant 
to decay, and 3) maturing/curing, during which the final product of the 
process yields a material that consists of organic and inorganic forms of 
carbon that are in pseudo-equilibrium leading to a low rate of carbon 
mineralization. The time frames for these phases of composting can 
range from several months to years depending on the type of system, the 
intensity of management, aeration conditions, and environmental con­
ditions (Bidlingmaier et al., 2004) (Table 1). 
3.1. Composting system design overview 
There are different methods used for composting, from large-scale 
windrow systems that are open to the environment, commonly found 
in municipal systems, to closed in-vessel systems that having some type 
of housing or container offered in a variety of sizes and requiring a 
reduced footprint. There are four main types of composting systems that 
are conventionally used, these being windrows, aerated static piles, 
enclosed channel, and in-vessel systems, with a fifth less common sys­
tem, hyperthermophilic composting (Campbell and Glasser, 2009; 
Grunklee, 1998). Management objectives for processing organic wastes 
through composting include: conversion of biologically unstable organic 
wastes into more stable forms, reduction of the volume of organic waste, 
improving sanitation conditions through the elimination of pathogens or 
hazardous chemicals (hyperthermophilic), and retention of nutrients in 
organic forms for reuse in agricultural or other production systems 
(Groat, 2017; Rihani et al., 2010). Composting systems, regardless of 
their scale, aim to provide an environment where aerobic decomposition 
is optimized to produce a stable end-product. Composting systems in­
crease in technical complexity from windrows to in-vessel systems 
because of the level of external controls imposed on the process, such as 
active aeration, automated control of mixing, enclosures that monitor 
and manage temperature, and leachate collection. 
3.1.1. Open composting systems 
Open composting systems are open to the atmosphere and subject to 
the effects of the local climate, seasons and weather, impacting the rate 
of decomposition and time to achieve a stabilized homogenous compost 
product but can be conducted at a large scale (Composting Council of 
Canada, 2006). 
3.1.2. Windrow composting systems 
Windrow systems are systems open to the environment and are 
mechanically mixed using tractor drawn windrow turners or front-end 
loading buckets. The mechanical mixing is conducted to enhance aera­
tion, increase exposure of labile components located on the outside of 
the pile, and to manage temperatures. The length of compost windrows 
is typically determined by site and management factors, providing sig­
nificant flexibility to compost facilities, but width and height are 
generally each restricted to 3–4 m for effective composting and easy 
access by normal farm equipment (CH2M HILL, 2013; Composting 
Council of Canada, 2006; Groat, 2017). Windrow composting is heavily 
dependent on operator skill, with operators monitoring the pile and 
turning as necessary, with more frequent turnings leading to a faster 
composting process. These systems are suitable for facilities receiving 
very high volumes of heterogeneous feedstocks on a regular basis, i.e. 
large municipal facilities, and where a large land footprint is available. 
3.1.3. Aerated static piles 
Aerated static pile composting is comprised of single batch-based 
trapezoidal piles of heterogeneous feedstocks. Depending on the feed­
stock type, the materials may be layered, such as in animal mortalities, 
or mixed prior to establishing the structure. These compost systems may 
be aerated passively or by forcing (positive) or pulling (negative) air 
through the trapezoidal compost pile. Once established, agitation only 
occurs when piles are combined or moved to a different area for curing. 
Piles often are covered with a layer of finished compost or wood chips, 
which are then incorporated when the piles are moved. Static aerated 
piles are generally used for biosolids, animal mortalities, or feedstocks 
that have similar consistency. Enclosed aerated static piles operate 
under the same principles as aerated static piles but are enclosed pre­
dominantly by plastic permeable membranes or organic matter is bag­
ged and forced air is applied(CH2M HILL, 2013; Composting Council of 
Canada, 2006; Groat, 2017). Aerated systems are typically found in the 
agricultural sector for management of crop residues or animal mortal­
ities. They are established as a low maintenance and relatively low-cost 
management system. 
3.1.4. Closed composting systems 
Closed composting systems are those not open to the atmosphere and 
are either covered or contained or enclosed within a structure or vessel. 
These systems often incorporate mechanical components for mixing and 
managing the decomposition process resulting in a shorter time-frame 
for decomposition (Composting Council of Canada, 2006). 
3.1.5. Enclosed composting systems 
Older in-vessel composting systems, referred to as in-vessel bays, 
enclosed halls, or silos, are typically an enclosed or semi-enclosed 
building with mechanical components that move material from one 
end of the structure to the other. Enclosed channel systems operate using 
mechanical agitation, with organic matter placed in long channels with 
concrete walls. Turning machines are utilized to agitate the material and 
move it from one end of the system to another, where the decomposition

3.1.6. Vertical in-vessel silo systems 
Vertical in-vessel silo systems offer a passive aeration system for 
cage-suspended organic matter within a silo vessel. These systems have 
several challenges related to odour control, build up of leachate in air- 
flow channels, and the slow rate of decomposition due to lack of 
agitation. Modified systems include the incorporation of aeration tubes 
within the piles to aid in passive aerations. Critical to these systems is the 
homogeneity of the feedstock with regards to particle size to ensure an 
even composting process, therefore rotary drum systems may be utilized 
as a pre-treatment. 
3.1.7. Modular in-vessel systems 
Modular in-vessel composting systems are fully enclosed and auto­
mated systems requiring direct energy input and are designed to provide 
optimal composting conditions and feedstock homogenization, resulting 
in an accelerated degradation process. The resulting output depends on 
the system type, material retention time, feedstock types, and operating 
conditions. The product may potentially require additional curing for a 
final, stable product. In-vessel composting systems are more expensive 
based on initial equipment requirements but require a smaller land base 
and range in processing capacity (CH2M HILL, 2013; Composting 
Council of Canada, 2006; Groat, 2017; Tetra Tech EBA inc., 2014). 
Modular in-vessel composting may include environmental monitoring, 
gas and temperature sensors, and mechanical mixing of material, along 
with active aeration to provide more control over the composting pro­
cess (Fig. 3). 
Small modular in-vessel systems are designed to process 10 tonnes 
per annum, medium systems can typically process up to 100 tonnes per 
annum and large systems can process approximately 5,000tonnes per 
annum. Processing capacity for many of these modular systems can be 
scaled up through addition of modules. Scaling up from small systems to 
large systems results in increasing complexity with respect to system 
design, operational input, running costs (e.g. electricity consumption), 
and logistics (Tetra Tech EBA inc., 2014). 
These systems can take up a significantly lower footprint than open 
composting systems but require higher initial capital investments. 
Different systems may have a fixed capacity with limited scalability, 
while other provide some flexibility on control of processing capacity to 
allow for increased handling and processing capacity through the 
installation of additional modules. A number of in-vessel designs and 
flow through approaches are currently on the market ranging from non- 
mechanically agitated (static) in-vessel containerized composting sys­
tems to fully automated turned and air injected systems at a range of 
different scales and configurations allowing for stand-alone or multi- 
unit integration (Engineered Compost Systems, 2019; Green Mountain 
Technologies, n.d.; Brome Compost Inc., 2019; Renewable Carbon 
Management LLC, 2019; Global Composting Solutions Ltd., n.d.). 
Different composting approaches have advantages and disadvantages 
that come with the operational goals and anticipated valorization 
pathways (Table 2). 
4. Cycling of carbon from organic wastes during composting 
Conventional practice in composting aims to generate a biologically 
stable compost end product. Managing the entire process for extraction 
of valuable products, such as gas and heat, is a fundamental shift in the 
organic waste management paradigm. This paradigm shifts includes 
capture and recycling of process by-products from composting such as 
energy and CO2. Transforming the model of composting operations to­
ward a circularized management approach requires: 1) identification of 
sinks for heat and CO2, 2) quantifying heat and CO2 generation per mass 
unit of organic matter, 3) design and integration of capture and distri­
bution systems for heat and gas, and 4) optimization of composting 
technologies for whole system recapture of resources. This review will 
focus on the generation and recycling of CO2 gas from composting as a 
valorization pathway. 
4.1. Factors influencing CO2 production in composts 
CO2 emissions are a recognised metabolic by-product of the com­
posting process, along with other gases including, NOX, NH4 and CH4. 
Gas type or composition is generally managed through effective com­
posting, maintaining aerobic conditions, anaerobic by-products and 
feedstock selection. In most circumstances, the goal in composting is to 
generate a stabilized organic matter product and therefore, little 
research is conducted on the quantification and capture of CO2 for use. 
Several variables influence the rate and quantity of CO2 produced over 
the active composting phase. The main factors ensuring successful 
composting and CO2 production are C:N ratio, moisture content, oxygen 
availability, temperature, and pre-processing (i.e., grinding, homoge­
nizing, particle size). Other factors impacting CO2 generation include 
mixing rate, bulking agents, compost amendments, and feedstock 
composition.

4.1.1. Feedstock composition 
Most of the composting studies identified through this review focus 
on agricultural manures or sewage sludge as a basis for nitrogen 
amendments, with a smaller number of studies focused on green waste 
(Table 3). The majority of carbon feedstocks in compost originate from 
wood shavings or sawdust, straw, rice husks, and hay. It is under­
standable that agricultural waste and sewage sludge predominate in the 
literature given the scale of the agricultural sector, followed by munic­
ipal green waste. Maximising CO2 emissions, or identifying the potential 
for CO2 emissions from specific sectors where high volumes of waste can 
be found (e.g., cannabis production, vertical farms or CEA) is an area 
requiring further research. Feedstock composition, both physical and 
chemical, drives chemical reaction rates and pre-processing re­
quirements (i.e. grinding, chopping) through availability of labile car­
bon and nitrogen. Physical properties of feedstocks will influence 
surface area reactions, retention of water, and porosity. 
4.1.2. Influence of amendments on CO2 production 
A number of studies have evaluated the influence on gas emissions 
from compost systems using supplements or microbial inoculants to 
common compostable material (Chen et al., 2015; Mao et al., 2019; Peng 
et al., 2019; Ren et al., 2019). The primary focus of these studies has 
been to evaluate the influence of supplements or amendments on mi­
crobial activity, to improve the fertilizing quality of the finished 
compost (generally total nitrogen content), to reduce GHG emissions, or 
a combination of the three. Studies have ranged across both open 
(windrow or static pile systems) and closed (in-vessel, or lab-scale bio­
reactors) systems. 
Bean dregs, a by-product of tofu production, are classified as a ni­
trogen rich material with large particle sizes and poor storage proper­
ties, making it a useful material for compost addition. Yang et al. (2020) 
evaluated the impact of bean dregs as an amendment to pig manure 
composting with the purpose of evaluating the carbon and nitrogen 
losses through GHG emission. Using wheat straw and pig manure as the 
carbon and nitrogen sources, bean dregs were added to the compost 
material using lab-scale 130L bioreactors (in-vessel system) and com­
posted for 56 days, with weekly turning. CO2 emissions peaked within 
the first 10 days of the composting process, 393.3 g CO2 d−1 for the 
amended compost mixture, and 352.7–393.3 g CO2 d−1 for the control. 
The cumulative CO2 emissions over 56 days were 4291 g CO2 for the 
bean dreg supplemented compost and 3432 g CO2 for the control (Yang 
et al., 2020). In another study, Mao et al. (2019) evaluated the impact of 
several supplements to pig manure compost, with the aim of evaluating 
changes in microbial communities within the compost and the impact on 
greenhouse gas emissions (Mao et al., 2019). Using sawdust and pig 
manure as the carbon and nitrogen sources, they evaluated the impact of 
zeolite, medical stone (a mineral additive), and wood vinegar. The study 
used small-scale glass bottle reactors and ran for 36 days with CO2 
emissions peaking on day 9, at 37.9 g CO2 d−1 for medical stone, 32.8 g 
CO2 d−1 for zeolite and 40.9 g CO2 d−1 for wood vinegar. Peng et al. 
(2019) also evaluated the impact of zeolite, as well as superphosphate, 
combined with ferrous sulfate on GHG emission reductions and im­
provements to fertilizer efficiency from composted chicken manure with 
rice husks under windrow composting. Using a standard windrow 
composting system, turned every two days for the first 15 days followed 
by 31 days of no turning, they identified peak CO2 emissions during the 
first 15 day phase, with an increase in oxygen diffusion and the pro­
motion of aerobic decomposition caused by the turning process. The 
addition of superphosphate reduced cumulative CO2 emissions by 
27.6% compared to the control. Another study evaluating addition of 
impact of superphosphate with pig manure also reported reductions in 
O2 consumption and CO2 production, with a mean production rate of 
14.05 g CO2 kg−1 DM d−1 and 20.6 g CO2 kg−1 DM d−1 for amended 
superphosphate and the control, respectively (Zhang et al. 2017). On a 
cumulative basis, the control treatment, using no amendments, from 
Zhang et al. (2017) produced 358.27 g CO2 kg−1 compared to the su­
perphosphate amended compost generating only 234.94 g CO2 kg−1. 
Chung et al. (2018) evaluated the influence of another clay additive to a 
wheat straw and poultry manure compost on GHG emissions, reporting 
reduced N2O and CH4 with increasing ratios of clay additive (DM basis) 
to compost but CO2 emissions also increased. These studies were con­
ducted using bench-top aerobic bioreactors over a 50-day period with 
mixing occurring after the 3rd day then every 7th day. Other studies 
have evaluated diatomite as a means to reduce GHG emissions (Ren 
et al. 2019). Using sawdust and poultry manure, their study added 5% 
and 10% diatomite (DM basis) to the compost mixture and composted 
the materials in a closed 80L container. Cumulative CO2 emissions were 
found to be 1421.04 g, 1587.06 g, and 1840.44 g for the control, for 5% 
diatomite, and 10% diatomite, respectively. The addition of diatomite 
increased CO2 production by 11.7% and 29.5%, and resulted in re­
ductions of gases with higher global warming potential than CO2, 
namely NH3, N2O, and CH4 (Ren et al., 2019). 
The addition of different reactive substrates can impact microbial 
processes occurring within the composting system, influencing the 
quantity and nature of gases produced. Reflecting on capturing CO2 
emissions from closed aerobic bioreactors, the use of select amendments 
may be a valuable method of influencing the rate and yield of CO2 gas, 
while simultaneously reducing other GHG emissions. Based on a review 
of the literature, there still is a clear need to evaluate the use of 
amendments in more detail, identify regionally accessible amendments, 
and conduct studies at a larger scale. Increasing CO2 levels while 
reducing other greenhouse gases has a two-fold benefit: 1) higher 
quantities of CO2 for capture and utilization, and 2) reduce the burden 
on gas filtration systems, thereby lowering operational costs.

4.1.4. Novel approaches impacting CO2 emissions 
There have been a limited number of novel approaches that have 
been applied to composting systems, with the aim to improving compost 
maturity and reducing GHG emissions. Tang et al. (2019) developed a 
process of electric-field-assisted aerobic composting. In this process, a 
direct-current was applied to the compost system with the purpose of 
using the electric current to increase O2 uptake. The theory relates to the 
production of electrons during microbial activity (respiration), with O2 
being the terminal acceptor. Oxygen is a vital component of the com­
posting process, however Tang et al. (2019) identified low efficiency of 
O2 transmission as the source of longer processing times between the 
start and finish of the decomposition process and generation of high 
levels of GHG emissions. Their research utilized bench-top aerobic 
bioreactors, with rice hulls, sewage sludge and poultry manure as the 
feedstock with a 2VoltDCapplied to the compost. The outcome was that 
levels of N2O and CH4were reduced with an electric field compared to 
the control treatment but an increase in CO2, 86.69 g CO2 kg−1 DM 
d−1and 55.01 g CO2 kg−1 DM d−1, for the treatment and control, 
respectively, occurred during the peak CO2 output on day 4. 
4.2. CO2 generation in field-based composting systems 
In a comprehensive meta-analysis of 88 studies focused on emissions 
from dairy manure composting (silo, static, turning and windrow), Ba 
et al. (2020) reported CO2 emissions from silos ranging from 2.35 to 
206.68 g CO2 kg (DM)−1, from static systems ranging from −0.16 – 
318.06 g CO2 kg (DM)−1, from turning systems ranging from 0.47 to 
569.49 g CO2 kg (DM)−1, and from windrow systems ranging from 0.001 
to 270.49 g CO2 kg (DM)−1 (Fig. 4). Clemens and Cuhls (2003), in a 
study of four different large-scale aerobic MSW treatment facilities, 
found that CO2 production levels ranged from 12 to 185 kg Mg−1 of 
organic matter. They identified that the ranges in CO2 emissions were 
influenced by aeration practices, with inefficient aeration causing lower 
CO2 and NH4 emission levels and higher levels of CH4. Amlinger et al. 
(2008) conducted a literature review on CO2 emissions from different 
composting systems (Amlinger et al., 2008) and identified ranges of 
25–383 kg CO2 Mg−1 of fresh organic matter. Across all composting 
methods, operational influences and external variables resulted in the 
high range in gas emissions (Amlinger et al., 2008). Moreover, Amlinger 
et al. (2008) conducted several trials evaluating GHG emissions (CO2, 
CH4, NH3 and N2O), comparing a windrow-based system with three 
different substrates: green waste and sewage sludge and a backyard 
composting system using garden waste. They reported a range between 
43 and 115 kg CO2 Mg−1 of fresh material (bio-waste), 118–194 kg CO2 
Mg−1 of fresh material (garden waste) and 10–25 kg CO2 Mg−1 of fresh 
material (sewage sludge). Lou and Nair (2009) evaluated GHG emissions 
from landfilling and composting and concluded that the majority of 
compost research has focused on improving production processes, fac­
tors relating to the quality of the finished product, and methods for 
improving quality but very little research has focused on process emis­
sions (Lou and Nair, 2009). Over the period between 2010 and 2019, 
there have been a range of studies evaluating total GHG emissions from 
composting systems to quantify emissions for use in Life Cycle Analysis, 
(Ahn et al., 2011; Gabriel et al., 2015; Jiang et al., 2011; Luo et al., 2014; 
S´anchez-Monedero et al., 2010).

4.3. Peak CO2 emissions 
CO2 emissions from composting systems are directly correlated to the 
temperature increase within the system. As microbial activity increases, 
compost temperature goes up resulting from released metabolic heat 
during decomposition. Han et al. (2018) identified peak CO2 concen­
trations at the transition point of the system from the mesophilic phase 
to the thermophilic phase, correlating to the increase in microbial ac­
tivity within the pile (Han et al., 2018). This transitional phase occurs as 
mesophilic microbial groups die releasing their labile components to the 
system and specialized thermophilic bacteria and fungi become domi­
nant and take over metabolic roles for decomposition of more recalci­
trant carbon forms (Antunes et al., 2016). Peak CO2 emissions typically 
occur between 2 and 12 days after mixing the feedstocks together, which 
aligns with the time frame for transition between the mesophilic phase 
to a thermophilic phase of composting. A review of 41 individual ex­
periments over 15 separate studies (Fig. 6) on both open and closed 
composting systems, peak CO2 emissions occurred early in the com­
posting process, with an average of 7.6 days in open systems and an 
average of 5.2 days in closed systems (Table 4). 
Extracting CO2 from an in-vessel composting system when the sys­
tem reaches peak or near peak CO2 emissions would be advantageous to 
maximize available gas for enrichment practises in CEA or vertical farm 
systems. This is analogous to the operation of continuous anaerobic 
digestion systems for biogas extraction (Hilkia et al., 2009) or the 
continuous operation of an algae bio-reactor (Croughan et al., 2015). 
Under continuous flow composting systems, the aim is to maintain 
constant operation of the system and receive a continuous output 
(Croughan et al., 2015). Batch systems aim to process a defined quantity 
of material that may be generated at specific times, i.e. crop harvest, 
rather than materials that are generated daily, i.e., manures or MSW 
organics. Vergara and Silver (2019) monitored GHG emissions contin­
uously from a windrow compost of animal manure, reporting 50% of the 
cumulative CO2 emissions over the first 21 days but steadily declining 
until the end of the 91 day study. Currently there are limited available 
studies on continuous CO2 monitoring from continuous in-vessel com­
posting systems. 
5. Application of in-vessel composting system to a vertical 
farming production framework 
Closed bioreactor or contained in-vessel systems will have different 
CO2 distribution patterns from open windrow or static pile systems. 
Windrow systems may be turned on a weekly or bi-weekly basis 
depending on height, width, porosity, heat profile, and moisture con­
tent, creating a variation in CO2 distribution in the system and off- 
gassing from the central column. In-vessel systems are turned on a 
more frequent basis, creating an even distribution of microbial activity, 
and more consistent rates of CO2 generation. Integration of in-vessel and 
other alternative systems with vertical farming is currently being 
demonstrated through the use of aquaponics; whereby wastewater from 
land-based aquaculture systems provide a nutrient solution for plant 
growth in CEA systems (Khandaker and Kotzen, 2018; K¨onig et al., 2016; 
Palm et al., 2018). Weidner et al. (2019) review the current knowledge, 
gaps, and outlooks for urban agriculture and argued for integration of 
decentralized anaerobic digestion and vermicomposting to address 
resource circularity in an urban production system. The current paper 
proposes the idea of resource circularity in vertical farming, similar to

The outputs of in-vessel composting systems including, CO2, heat, 
and stabilized compost product, have the potential to be integrated 
within vertical farming systems. Utilizing CO2 from compost processing 
to enrich plant production environments, heat to reduce energy con­
sumption, and compost product as seedling plug stocks or nursery media 
all have potential to reduce operating costs and improve circularity.

5.1. Elevated CO2 environments for plant production 
The concentration of CO2, as measured at the mid-troposphere (5 km 
from surface level), is 415 ppm (global average) (Change,N.G.C., n.d.),

spatially and temporally. García et al. (2008) monitored CO2 levels on a

fluctuations diurnally and seasonally with reduced levels of CO2 during 
the summer period. The concentration of CO2in different geographical 
areas is impacted by localized CO2 producing systems ranging from in­
dustrial operations, road traffic, and composting facilities. These sources 
are balanced by CO2 sinks including forestry cover, agricultural lands, 
green spaces and water bodies.

levels of CO2 are a limiting factor to plant growth, with plant CO2 
assimilation occurring during daylight hours when stomatal pores are 
open and gas exchange can occur. Even partially enclosed plant pro­
duction environments, in the absence of CO2 supplementation and under 
optimal growth conditions, can experience a decrease in CO2 levels as 
photosynthetic rates outpace restricted diffusion of ambient CO2. 
ResearchintoCO2 enrichment of greenhouses fluctuated repeatedly be­
tween the 1950’s and 1980’s (Mortensen, 1987) before more wide­
spread adoption and application occurred across Europe and the USA (Li 
et al., 2020). The use of CO2, through an enriched growing environment, 
is a method used by growers to improve yields through increased 
photosynthetic rates (Blom et al., 2002; Esmeijer and Bloem, 1999; 
Poudel and Dunn, 2017; Pratt et al., 2011). 
Traditionally, in controlled environment agriculture, ventilation is a 
key operational parameter used to: control temperature, manage hu­
midity levels and prevent condensation, and replenish CO2. During the 
dark period, CO2 levels increase due to plant and microbial respiration, 
which is not offset by light dependent photosynthesis reactions (Boulard 
et al., 1996; Iddio et al., 2020). In a study conducted by Frantz (2011), 
CO2 levels in a closed environment were recorded as low as 175 ppm due 
to plant drawdown from photosynthesis. Similarly, Poudel and Dunn 
(2017) reported concentration ranges of 150–200 ppm during day-time 
hours in sealed greenhouses, highlighting the importance of ventilation 
in CO2 replenishment. The addition of supplemental CO2 on plant 
growth and crop responses in greenhouses is well documented in the 
literature, from Mortensen’s substantial review (Mortensen, 1987) to 
Idso and Idso’s ten year review of Plant responses to atmospheric CO2 
enrichment in the face of environmental constraints: a review (Idso and Idso, 
1994). Blom et al. (2002) state that between 800 ppm and 1000 ppm is 
ideal for crops raised from seedlings including tomatoes, cucumbers, 
peppers and lettuce. At higher CO2 levels (e.g., >1000 ppm), there are 
diminishing returns as saturation can be reached depending on available 
light flux. 
Sources of CO2 for greenhouses and CEA, include: a) complete air 
turnover through effective ventilation, b) combustion of biomass and 
the capture and re-direction of flue gas, c) dedicated natural gas/pro­
pane burners, or d) from liquid/compressed CO2 tanks. Propane and 
natural gas are commonly combusted to produce CO2, providing 
approximately a 1:3 ratio of kilograms of hydrocarbon fuel source to 
CO2. Burners are placed directly in the growing space (greenhouse or 
vertical farm) and regulation of CO2 can be controlled through sensors 
and ventilation. For larger greenhouse systems or multiple greenhouses, 
CO2enrichment can be achieved using a gas engine (e.g. boiler) or 
Combined Heat and Power system with an integrated flue gas condenser 
(Blom et al., 2002; Esmeijer and Bloem, 1999; Poudel and Dunn, 2017; 
Pratt et al., 2011). By-products from the combustion of fossil-fuel 
derived CO2 also include heat and water. The production of heat in 
the winter-months can be viewed as a positive aspect to production, 
extending growing seasons in Northern climates but in the summer 
months heat removal becomes an additional cost to HVAC systems or 
other types of venting. Water production from these systems can also be 
controlled through increasing ventilation rates (Blom et al., 2002; 
Poudel and Dunn, 2017; Pratt et al., 2011). In contrast, liquid CO2 
removes the generation of heat and moisture by-products but increases 
the cost of the CO2 source. Liquid CO2 is more expensive per kg of gas 
compared to propane and natural gas (Blom et al., 2002; Poudel and 
Dunn, 2017; Pratt et al., 2011). Delivering CO2 to plants can involve 
either filling the growing space with elevated CO2 levels using a series of 
vents and ducts or delivering CO2 at plant level in a targeted manner 
using a network of tubing placed within the plant crop canopy. A tar­
geted approach to CO2 elevation using an in-canopy approach increases 
efficiency of gas uptake and ensures a more even distribution of CO2 
uptake within the crop (Blom et al., 2002; Esmeijer and Bloem, 1999; 
Pratt et al., 2011). Further, delivery of CO2 directly to the canopy aids in 
the management of water vapour/humidity within the canopy. 
In a review of technical reports on the use of CO2 enrichment, 
Nederhoff (1990) focused on enrichment using flue gas capture/direct 
combustion using burners and liquid CO2. Other reports have evaluated 
technical and economic conditions of using flue gas capture and direct 
combustion using burners (Esmeijer and Bloem, 1999). The common 
theme in these reports is the use of combustion for the production of 
CO2, with Pratt et al. (2011) stating that economically, the generation of 
CO2 from composting does not compare economically to other forms of 
CO2 generation. They provided an estimate of CAD$170 t−1 CO2 using 
composting methods compared to CAD$110t−1 CO2 using a natural gas 
boiler system and capturing the flue gas. The costs were conditional on 
the existing cost of straw and natural gas. Moreover, the report did not 
take into account potential costs of waste disposal or carbon use from 
fossil fuels. Oklahoma State University Extension Service, produced a 
factsheet on CO2 supplementation (Poudel and Dunn, 2017) briefly 
discussing the use of decomposition and fermentation methods for CO2 
generation, referencing several commercial options including the “CO2 
Boost Bucket”, “Pro CO2” and Exhale” mushroom bag, all of which are 
small scale and designed with the residential greenhouse market in 
mind. Poudel and Dunn (2017) highlighted the possibility of using 
decomposing organic matter in buckets to provide CO2 but identified the 
need for significant amounts of substrate and physical space for storage. 
5.2. Composting as a source of CO2 enrichment of plant production 
environments 
Conceptually, the use of compost to produce CO2 for plant growth is 
a recognised option but has not been attempted at a commercial level 
and only a small number of studies have evaluated this option. Concerns 
from commercial operations relate to: a) uncertainty of cost, potential 
introduction of odours, pests, or pathogens, b) unwanted gases from the 
composting process, c) excess moisture or heat, d) leachate manage­
ment, e) requirements for additional space, and f) management of the 
composting process (Esmeijer and Bloem, 1999; Province of Manitoba, 
2021). There are a limited number of studies that have recently evalu­
ated the potential of CO2 production from compost in a plant production 
environment. Karim et al. (2020) evaluated CO2 enrichment using crop 
residues and animal manure (CRAM) using five 0.34 m3 vessels in a 753 
m3 sized greenhouse. They demonstrated both a sustained increase in 
CO2 levels and an increase in plant productivity, with greater crop yields 
of tomatoes compared to the control greenhouse system. Hao et al. 
(2004) conducted a similar study finding a sustained increase in CO2 and 
an increase in tomato yields compared to the ambient control. 
5.3. Challenges of using CO2 derived from composting systems 
Technical barriers need to be overcome in order to transition CEA 
and vertical farm systems into a recapture and recycling model for their 
organic wastes. In particular, composting systems generate a gas 
mixture that may include components, such as methane, ethylene, 
ammonia, which are harmful to plant growth and development. More­
over, CEA production systems must meet different regulatory criteria 
with respect to biosecurity, pathogen management, and hygiene that 
field-based systems are not bound by. This will mean additional research 
on management of bioaerosols, particulate matter, pathogens, and 
handling of organic waste materials during the composting process to 
recapture and recycle CO2. Each of these technical barriers has the po­
tential to adversely impact plant production, human health, and oper­
ations of CEAs.

5.3.2. Moisture removal 
Moisture removal from composting gas streams are achieved through 
a condensation trap, whereby the output gas is passed through a heat 
exchange. This is a method currently built into in-vessel composting 
systems including those by Green Mountain Technologies, Brome 
Compost Inc., and Global Composting Solutions Ltd. A condensation 
trap method may not be sufficient for all CEA systems requiring a sec­
ondary process of solid desiccant dehydration. This process would use 
common absorbents including silica gel, activated alumina, or activated 
carbon in a column trap to remove excess moisture (Speight, 2017; 
Stewart, 2014). The disadvantage of this approach is a requirement for 
periodic maintenance of the absorbent via drying to recharge and cost. 
5.3.3. Compost gas mixtures 
Gas outputs from composting, while predominantly comprising of 
CO2 can also contain ammonia (NH3), methane (CH4), and nitrous oxide 
(N2O) (Ma et al., 2020). NH3, CH4 and N2O emissions cannot be directly 
eliminated but can concentrations can be reduced through management 
of air flow, feedstock selection, adjusting process or retention time, and 
the use of filtration or traps. There are a range of different methods 
currently used for removing CO2 from gas streams, particularly biogas 
from anaerobic digestion and natural gas. These methods include the use 
of water scrubbing, amine scrubbing, pressure swing adsorption and 
membrane filtrations (Petersson, 2013; Sun et al., 2015; Wasajja et al., 
2020). Both water scrubbing and amine scrubbing operate under the 
principle that CO2 is absorbed by a liquid (CO2 chemically reacting with 
the scrubbing solvent or CO2 dissolving into water) and separated from 
the desired gas of CH4 before being stripped from the solvent/water 
(Yang and Li, 2014). Pressure swing adsorption (PSA) and membrane 
filtration are dry systems. PSA systems uses a series of vessels operating 
under pressure and uses an adsorptive media to separate out the gas, 
(CO2, CH4) CO2 is preferentially adsorbed due to smaller molecular size 
compared to CH4, allowing CH4 to be removed. After CH4 has been 
removed, the system is depressurised allowing the captured gas to be 
released (CO2). Membrane filtration requires the parent gas, under 
pressure, to be streamed through a single or a series of polymeric 
membranes to separate out CO2 from CH4. This occurs due to CO2 being 
an ionically charged small molecule, allowing the CH4 to pass through 
the membrane, creating two separate gas streams (Greene, 2018; Yang 
and Li, 2014).

the composting system will vary according to the size of the production 
operation, i.e., quantity of biomass generated and feed-in rate, and type 
of composting system. For example, a 125W blower operating on a 24hr 
basis at a rate of $0.174kwh−1 would result in a daily running cost of 
$0.53, comparable to Natural Gas. For a one-tier Grow Box production 
system, $1.06 per day for aeration could be expected based on two 
composting units. 
Integration of in-vessel composting-vertical farming systems has the 
potential to push the boundary of resource circularity, especially when 
applied to restaurants that are looking for local supplies of fresh pro­
duce. Fueling the in-vessel composting system with organic residues 
from the vertical farm (cutting down external waste disposal costs) has 
cost reduction opportunities and benefits for plant production. Long- 
term prospects from circularizing CO2 enrichment may also include 
carbon credits and energy-offsets from reduced transportation of foods 
from local vertical farm production. Another valorization opportunity 
may be in using the compost products to extract additional fertility for 
nutrient solutions or converting the stabilized compost into a seedling 
plug stock alternative media, however, these are still areas requiring 
further research. 
6.2. Future direction and recommendations 
The capture of CO2 from industrial processes including fertiliser 
plants (Haugen et al., 2017; Zhuang et al., 2012) is well documented, as 
is the technology for CO2 scrubbing (Chung et al., 2018; Kr´otki et al., 
2020; Kapoor et al., 2021). Composting systems can produce CO2 
emissions at levels that may meet requirements of small to medium sized 
plant production systems. Pilot studies evaluating integration of biore­
actor systems with CEA facilities will be needed to determine how fac­
tors such as mixed gas scrubbing, bioaerosol filtering, and biosecurity of 
the production system can be optimized. As the concept of circular 
economies or closed loop systems grows, more industries and infra­
structure will be built around resource integration and development of 
valorization opportunities (Malolan et al., 2021; Calone et al., 2019; 
Nuwansi et al., 2021). There is a clear lack of research focusing on the 
valorization and use of metabolic by-products from aerobic composting 
as part of a circularization strategy in CEA and vertical farm systems. 
Growing investments in CEA and vertical farming creates new synergies 
to process residual biomass, i.e., non-harvestable residues, using com­
posting approaches for recapture and utilization of carbon that can be 
recycled back into plant production. With an increased desire for local 
produce by consumers, chefs, and restaurants there are clear opportu­
nities for vertical farming systems to include in-vessel composting to 
circularize resources and reduce costs (Dukeshire et al., 2011; Murphy 
and Smith, 2009; Roy and Ballantine, 2020; Zhang et al., 2020). 
In-vessel composting systems are not only likely to become more inte­
gral and valuable in achieving successful waste management objectives; 
more crucially they are a key component of zero waste principles.

7. Conclusions 
The aim of this review is to highlight the potential scope for recap­
turing and recycling metabolic CO2 gas from composting of residual 
biomass and organic wastes in CEA or vertical farm systems. Composting 
has generated significant research interest and outcomes over the past 
few decades as a waste management tool. This paper has identified some 
of the opportunities for CO2 generation from composting systems to 
meet the needs of small-scale commercial CEA systems and vertical 
farms. The current gaps in knowledge that will need additional research 
include quantifying CO2 from the non-edible biomass of different CEA 
systems and vertical farms for use in continuous feed-in compost sys­
tems, developing models of compositional analysis of compost gases, 
heat valorization and management, development of compost media as a 
seedling plug stock alternative, quantification of nutrient content from 
compost for reuse in hydroponic solutions, and operational life cycle 
analyses of CEA-composting in integrated systems.
