A B S T R A C T
For this study, a controlled and monitored bioreactor was constructed to examine the environmental variables, 
CO2 behavior during the biodegradation process, aeration requirements, and their effect on the growth of black 
soldier fly larvae. The larvae were fed with bovine and ovine manure residues, as well as mixtures of agave 
bagasse and vinasse, which are by-products of mezcal distillation, at feeding rates of 47.67, and 95.23 mg/larvae. 
day. The larvae with the highest growth were those fed on sheep manure, while the larvae with the lowest 
growth were fed on the mixture of agave bagasse and vinasse. The nutritional content of the frass was analyzed, 
and C:N ratios of 18.75 and 10 were obtained for the fertilizers derived from bovine and ovine manure, 
respectively, though phosphorus concentrations were low. Data from the sensors validated the monitoring sys­
tem and data acquisition, enabling the establishment of aeration requirements based on CO2 concentration and 
projecting mathematical models to complement this study.
1. Introduction
The sustainable management of agro-industrial and organic wastes 
has become a global challenge due to the quantity, diversity and 
complexity of the materials generated. Among these, animal manure, 
sludge from wastewater treatment plants, distillery residues, brewery 
by-products, fruit pulps, lignocellulosic bagasse such as agave and sug­
arcane bagasse, and other agricultural residues rich in cellulose, hemi­
cellulose and lignin represent a major environmental impact. Despite 
efforts to implement traditional techniques such as composting, vermi­
composting and direct soil application, these methods face limitations 
due to the presence of heavy metals, recalcitrant compounds and high 
operating costs. In this context, the use of insects such as Hermetia illu­
cens emerges as an innovative biotechnological alternative, capable of 
efficiently reducing waste volumes while producing biomass rich in 
proteins, lipids and other valuable compounds. The incorporation of 
bioreactors designed to monitor and optimize parameters such as tem­
perature, humidity and gaseous emissions reinforces this strategy, 
providing a more controlled, efficient and sustainable approach for the 
valorization of organic waste and the mitigation of its environmental 
impacts.

1.2. Waste from the distilling industry
Other wastes with valorization potential include those from the 
alcoholic fermentation process. The distillery industry is among the 17 
most polluting industries in the world (Dhote et al., 2021). These in­
dustries not only produce effluents with high organic matter concen­
trations, such as BOD levels between 45 000 and 60 000 mg/L (Mohana 
et al., 2009), but also generate pressed sludge with no direct industrial 
application (Fito et al., 2017). In the EU alone, approximately 3.4 
million tons of solid waste are produced annually, including yeast and 
cereal residues, which are used as animal feed ingredients (Aliyu and 
Bala, 2011). Of the sludge generated, 80 % is disposed of in landfills 
after dewatering, which poses significant environmental problems. 
Landfills contribute to soil, air, and water pollution, including the 
release of harmful gases from untarred sludge (Ferronato and Torretta, 
2019).
Distilleries generate three types of solid waste: bagasse, distillate 
residues that vary depending on the raw materials used (yeast sludge), 
and wastewater treatment sludge, which is considered non-usable ma­
terial. In terms of liquid waste, stillages are produced at a rate of 1 to 6 
liters for every liter of finished product (S. P. Wang et al., 2022). 
Additionally, in beer production, wastewater is generated from washing 
spent malt grains, which also has valorization potential. However, the 
industry faces resistance in adopting valorization methods. Currently, 
alternatives include composting, although this has limitations due to the 
waste’s pH and its content of organic substances and metals (Mahaly 
et al., 2018). Some small companies use these wastes for crop irrigation, 
leading to soil contamination, while decomposition in landfills con­
tributes to an increased carbon footprint.
1.3. Agave bagasse in mezcal production
In Mexico, a prominent distillery industry is the production of 
mezcal, made from various species of the genus Agave, commonly 
known as maguey. The production of mezcal from species such as A. 
angustifolia, A. potatorum, A. karwinskii, and A. marmorata generates an 
average of 5.63 to 12.83 kg of bagasse per liter of mezcal, with an 
average of 9 kg/L depending on the milling and distillation processes 
(Flores-Ríos et al., 2020). In 2021, 8.1 million liters of mezcal were 
produced. In Puebla alone, 278 625.93 liters of mezcal were produced in 
2022, representing 3.44 % of national production, while bagasse gen­
eration reached approximately 4179 tons (Consejo Mexicano and Reg­
ulador de la Calidad del Mezcal A.C., 2023). Agave bagasse is a 
lignocellulosic residue composed of 43 % cellulose, 19 % hemicellulose, 
and 15 % lignin by dry weight (Saucedo-Luna et al., 2011).
The application of untreated sludge to soil reduces manganese 
availability, increases soil alkalinity, and inhibits seed germination 
(Parvathi and Hemanth Kumar, 2014). The Maillard reaction occurs 
between amino groups and carbonyl groups, leading to the formation of 
melanoidins a dark brown pigment found in distillery effluent sludge 
and stillage (Chandra et al., 2008). Melanoidins inhibit the development 
of beneficial microorganisms in both soil and water.
1.4. Waste treatment methods
Sludge and residues can be treated using physical, chemical, 
biological, and thermal methods, including pyrolysis, dehydration, bri­
quetting, and composting, among others. However, the costs associated 
with these treatments can reduce producers interest. The most prom­
ising option is to use these materials for agricultural purposes; however, 
it is essential to analyze their toxicity, especially concerning their 
application to food crops (Mahaly et al., 2018).
Composting and vermicomposting are viable treatment options; 
however, they have limitations due to the presence of heavy metals in 
distillery sludge. These heavy metals can negatively impact the popu­
lation of microorganisms and microbial enzymes in vermicomposting 
(Antizar-Ladislao et al., 2010). Additionally, applying higher concen­
trations of distillery sludge to the soil can lead to a subsequent decrease 
in soil fertility.
Another alternative for waste treatment and nutrient valorization is 
the use of insects. Processed animal protein (PAP) obtained from seven 
insect species can be utilized as feed for non-ruminant animals, 
including fish, pigs, and poultry, as approved by the European Com­
mission (Beyers et al., 2023).
1.5. Use of black soldier flies in waste valorization
Hermetia illucens is a dipteran fly belonging to the family Strat­
iomyidae, found in tropical, subtropical, and warm regions of the 
Americas (Z. Liu et al., 2020). Adult flies do not have stingers, mouth­
parts, or digestive organs. Males have a golden abdomen, while females 
are brown in color, with a body size ranging from 15 to 20 mm in length 
(Kumar et al., 2018). Females mate two days after emerging and can 
produce between 206 and 639 eggs in a single clutch (Tomberlin et al., 
2002).
The maturation process of Hermetia illucens takes two months with an 
efficient substrate source and up to four months in the presence of 
nutritional deficiencies (Makkar et al., 2014). Under laboratory condi­
tions with temperatures between 27 and 28 ◦C, development from egg to 
prepupa takes 22 to 24 days, while the complete development from egg 
to adult requires 40 to 43 days (Tomberlin et al., 2009). Larvae undergo 
six instars, corresponding to six molts, before pupation. In the sixth 
instar, they stop feeding and search for a dry place to transition to the 
adult stage. The instars can be identified by differences in the width of 
the head capsule (Giannetto et al., 2017).
Multiple investigations have reported that black soldier fly (BSF) 
larvae can grow on a wide variety of substrates, including plant mate­
rials, animal feces, tannin-containing substrates, and brewery waste, 
among others (Bonelli et al., 2019). According to Müller and collabo­
rators, BSF larvae produce enzymes that degrade cellulose, chitin, and 
lignin (Müller et al., 2017), demonstrating their effectiveness in the 
decomposition of fibrous materials.
One of the concerns regarding the use of insects is the potential 
proliferation of pathogens. However, the black soldier flies only feeds 
during its larvael stage due to its small oral structure (Li et al., 2017). 
These larvae require substrates with high moisture content, so they 
naturally proliferate in environments where manure and other organic 
remains are present. In this way, they biotransform waste into proteins 
and lipids, which they subsequently use to develop into adults (Van Huis 
et al., 2013).
Larvae can weigh up to 220 mg and feed rapidly on diets ranging 
from 25 to 500 mg of fresh matter per larvae per day (Makkar et al., 
2014). Giannetto et al. reported that larvae reached the second larvael 
stage in 4 days, with a mass of 91 mg and a size of 4 mm, while the fifth 
larvael stage was reached in 13 days, with a mass of 220 mg and a length 
of 14 mm (Giannetto et al., 2017). Studies on the average nutrient 
composition of Hermetia illucens larvae indicate that it is highly depen­
dent on their diet, with approximate compositions of carbohydrates (37 
%), chitin (9 %), and lipids (15 %), along with protein content (32 %) in 
dry mass (Singh and Kumari, 2019). Larvael survival to the prepupal 
stage is 96 ± 2 %, with an adult emergence rate of 21 to 27 % 
(Tomberlin et al., 2002).

1.6. Biotransformation efficiency indicators
Indicators used to evaluate the biotransformation capacity of BSF 
larvae include survival rate, bioconversion rate, waste conversion effi­
ciency, waste reduction rate, larval growth and nutrient content. Sur­
vival rate was established as a reference indicator in insect experiments 
by Oonincx and was subsequently used by Miranda, Myers, Lalander, 
Rehman and Gold (Liu et al., 2020; T. Liu et al., 2022; Miranda et al., 
2020; Myers et al., 2008; Lalander et al., 2019; Rehman et al., 2017; 
Gold et al., 2020a). According to Gold, the survival rate increases to 
between 97 % and 100 % when BSF larvae are fed mixtures of different 
organic waste streams, compared to individual waste sources (Siddiqui 
et al., 2022).
The bioconversion rate measures the yield of organic waste trans­
formation, relating the mass of the substrate to the final mass obtained 
by the larvae, as well as its composition in protein and fat. This rate can 
vary according to the composition of the feeding substrate (Gold et al., 
2018; Rehman et al., 2017). Bosh and collaborators indicate that this 
rate should be calculated on a dry mass basis. Another important indi­
cator is waste conversion efficiency (Z. Liu et al., 2018), also referred to 
as ingested/digested food conversion efficiency, which relates the 
amount of undigested substrate to the biomass produced (Diener et al., 
2009a; Gold et al., 2020b; Oonincx et al., 2015a; Villa et al., 2021).
Given these challenges, alternative methods that promote sustain­
able waste valorization are needed. Among these, Hermetia illucens has 
emerged as a promising biological agent for converting organic waste 
into valuable products such as proteins, lipids, and biofertilizers. This 
study aims to evaluate the potential of BSF larvae in bioreactors for the 
degradation of agricultural and industrial residues under controlled 
conditions, providing insights into nutrient recycling and the production 
of sustainable bioresources.
1.7. Bioreactors and their role in process optimization
To achieve optimal rearing conditions for black soldier fly (BSF) 
larvae, temperatures between 27 and 33 ◦C, along with appropriate 
relative humidity and aeration processes, are necessary (Myers et al., 
2008). Several authors have developed bioreactors for this purpose. For 
instance, Padmanabha and Streif designed a controlled environment 
reactor equipped with actuators for ventilation control, flow direction, 
lighting, and ambient temperature management, including cooling and 
heating. To facilitate water and nutrient exchange, they incorporated a 
circuit with five membrane pumps: two for gas inlet and outlet and three 
for water and nutrient inlet and outlet. Additionally, the system includes 
sensors for monitoring CO2, O2, and volatile organic compounds, as well 
as temperature and humidity. Performance tests of the sensors and 
microcontrollers were conducted as part of the study (Padmanabha and 
Streif, 2019).
Parodi and co-workers constructed 265 L open-circuit climatic 
breathing chambers (80 × 50 × 45 cm) with air temperature controlled 
at 27 ± 0.5 ◦C, relative humidity maintained at 70 ± 5 %, and a light (L) 
ratio of 1:23. The air flow rate was set at 27 L/min, and two fans were 
utilized to ensure adequate air mixing, resulting in air velocity varying 
from 0.2 to 0.8 m/s. A mass balance was calculated to quantify nutrient 
bioconversion efficiencies, including carbon, energy, nitrogen, phos­
phorus, and potassium, as well as gaseous emissions (CO2 equivalent 
and ammonia) (Parodi et al., 2020).
Lalander and co-workers utilized a reactor for active ventilation 
control within a 3.2 m³ chamber. Ventilation is provided by four fans 
(R¨orfl¨akt® ø100 mm), each with a flow rate of 107 m³/h, blowing air 
down through openings in the reactor chamber’s roof. Additionally, two 
fans (Ventilationsfl¨akt® ø 100 mm) with a flow rate of 187 m³/h each 
are connected to two exhaust air pipes. The reactor chamber is equipped 
with combined air temperature and humidity sensors (AM2302/DHT22, 
Adafruit®). The study found that active aeration can contribute to 
substrate water depletion. Without correcting for environmental 
temperature, larvae exhibited reduced growth and development 
(Lalander et al., 2021).
L. Cheng and co-workers developed a reactor to treat nitrogen- 
containing water using BSF larvae. The setup involved pumping water 
into the bioreactor for 1 min, followed by a 5-minute pause. The 
experiment was conducted in a constant temperature water bath set at 
30 ◦C for 7 days, with each group consisting of three biological repli­
cates. Nitrogen emissions, in the form of ammonia and N2O, were 
monitored to correlate their elimination. The results indicated a 
reduction of nitrogen species by up to 70 % (Chen et al., 2023). Bio­
reactors not only optimize larval growth, but also allow monitoring of 
gas emissions and evaluation of nutrient conversion efficiency.
The research compared the degradation of dairy cattle manure, 
sheep manure, agave residues, and vinasses from mezcal production, 
mediated by black soldier fly larvae, in a bioreactor. The setup included 
monitoring of ambient temperature, relative humidity, CO2, and 
pollutant gases. The growth of larvae fed the selected diets was evalu­
ated, along with the analysis of components related to soil nutrition, as 
well as the production of proteins and fats in the black soldier fly larvae, 
with the aim of producing fertilizers and biofuels.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Reactor design and construction at laboratory scale
The rearing process of black soldier fly larvae has specific re­
quirements that need to be controlled, such as ambient temperature, 
aeration, and substrate supply. The reactor is a controlled environment 
model, constructed from a 50 L polystyrene container, designed to hold 
up to 10 vessels with a maximum treatment capacity of 3000 g of black 
soldier fly larvae. While the dimensions of the vessels may vary, 8.5 × 14 
cm vessels were utilized.
Temperature and humidity are regulated by the STC 3028 device 
(operating range: −20 to 80 ◦C and 0 to 100 % RH), which integrates an 
electrical resistance heater and an ultrasonic humidifier (HUM-24 V, 15 
ml/h). The air inlet and outlet are managed by a double analog timer 
(EBCHQ®), and three Phoenix® air fans, each with a flow rate of 170 
m³/h and a speed of 3000 RPM, are included for aeration. The moni­
toring system consists of an ambient temperature and relative humidity 
sensor (DHT11), with an operating range of 0 to 50 ◦C and 20–95 % RH. 
It also includes a CO2 sensor (CCS811) with a sensitivity range of 400 to 
8192 ppm, and an air quality sensor (M135) that detects harmful gases. 
Additionally, four type K thermocouples (Max 6675) are employed for 
temperature verification in the substrate, with a measurement range of 
0 to 1024 ◦C (Fig. 1). The programming for the system was developed 
using Arduino® libraries.

The experimental design included feeding rates of 47.62 mg/lar­

feces, and mezcal process residues (vinasse and bagasse in proportions of

2.3. Chemical composition of substrates and larvae
2.3.1. Lignin determination method
Lignin is used in the Monod growth equation as a reference for the 
transformation of substrate to biomass and as a basis for establishing Ks 
(Wang and Witarsa, 2016). In the method used for lignin determination, 
samples of dry substrate and biomass (frass) were dried to a constant 
mass. Samples of 1 g were taken, and hydrolysis was carried out with 0.1 
M sulfuric acid in a water bath at 40 ◦C for 2 h. The mixture was then 
filtered under vacuum through a previously dried filter, which was 
weighed at 105 ◦C for 2 h. The percentage of lignin was obtained by the 
gravimetric method.
2.3.2. Total Nitrogen - protein content determination method (Kjendahl)
The equipment used for digestion was the Turbotherm, and for 
distillation, the Vapodest 30S from Gerhardt Analytic ®, utilizing the 
Kjeldahl method. For the substrate and frass samples, calcination was 
performed at 550 ◦C. The larvae samples were not calcined, and 0.5 g 
samples were weighed. In a digestion tube, 2 g of selenium catalyst re­
agent and 20 ml of concentrated sulfuric acid were added. The digestion 
was carried out at 420 ◦C until the sample became transparent.

sodium hydroxide to the digestion tube. In the Erlenmeyer flask, 10 ml 
of boric acid solution, along with methyl red and bromocresol green 
indicators, were added and titrated with 0.01 N sulfuric acid until the 
solution changed from green to pink (Jiang et al., 2014). Eq. (6) was

3. Results and discussion
Hermetia illucens larvae were supplied by the Universidad Aut´onoma 
de Quer´etaro. The larvae were 5 days old and had been fed chick feed 
since hatching.
The selected substrates for feeding the BSF larvae included bovine 
feces, ovine feces (obtained from CITAP located in Atlixco, Universidad 
Popular Aut´onoma del Estado de Puebla), and two mixtures of agave 
bagasse from the potatorum species and vinasse from the mezcal pro­
duction process (Escuela de Mezcal de Puebla).
3.1. Moisture of the substrate during the experiment
The required moisture conditions for the substrate must exceed 50 % 
(Cheng et al., 2017). For this reason, the experiments conducted with the 
agave and vinasse samples required the addition of water on day 11, 
while the sheep feces substrate experiment, with a feeding rate of 95.23 
mg/larvae.day, necessitated an increase in moisture on day 15. During 
the experiments, the moisture levels of the cattle and sheep feces sub­
strates with feeding rates of 47.61 mg/larvae.day remained constant. 
The bovine and ovine feces substrates with feeding rates of 95.23 
mg/larvae.day exhibited the highest moisture levels, with only minor 
fluctuations (Fig. 2).

3.3. Characterization of substrates and organic fertilizers
In the European Union, an organic solid fertilizer is defined as ma­
terial containing at least 1 % nitrogen or a sum of NPK content of at least 
4 %. The NPK ratio in organic fertilizers, as established by Regulation 
(EU) 2019/, is 10:4:6 (Fern´andez, 2022). According to standards in Asia, 
the nitrogen content in fertilizers should be higher than 1.5 % in 
Malaysia, 4 % in South Korea, and between 2.5 % to 5 % in the 
Philippines (Abd Manan et al., 2024). In the United States, plant or 
animal fertilizers must comply with a C:N ratio between 25:1 and 40:1 
(Makan, 2015). In Colombia, the recommended nitrogen content is at 
least 1 %, with a C:N ratio of less than 25:1 (Boh´orquez et al., 2015). 
However, the NPK ratio for black soldier fly larvae feces, as mentioned 
by G¨arttling & Schulz, corresponds to 1:0.9:1.1 (G¨arttling and Schulz, 
2022).
The results of the characterization of the substrates, larvaes BSF and

In the characterization performed by Gold on bovine manure, the

3.5. Validation of sensors in the bioreactor
3.5.1. Temperature and humidity sensors
To validate the behavior of the sensors installed in the bioreactor, the 
agave-vinasse bagasse test was conducted in Puebla, Mexico, where the 
minimum ambient temperature ranged from 9 ◦C to 22 ◦C. The sensors 
were programmed to collect data every minute, including a humidity 
and temperature sensor (DHT11), a CO2 sensor, an air quality sensor 
(M135), and four thermocouples inserted in a replica of the second trial.
According to the manufacturers of the sensors, heating of the system 
is necessary. Therefore, the STC 3028 device was programmed to turn 
the heater on below 27 ◦C and off at 29 ◦C. Additionally, the humidifier 
was programmed to activate below 50 % and deactivate at 65 % RH. The 
operation of the DHT11 sensor was verified, and the growth chamber 
reached a temperature of 27 ◦C by minute 133.
During the 21-day trial, the DHT11 sensor recorded an average 
temperature of 28.1 ◦C, with a minimum of 20.2 ◦C and a maximum of 
34.7 ◦C, effectively maintaining the necessary conditions for larvael 
growth (Fig. 5).
The K-type thermocouples (Max 6675) monitored the temperature of 
the substrates, which reached and maintained the target temperature 
after 16.5 h. It was noted that the temperature in the growth chamber 
decreased due to the reduction in ambient temperature at night.
The highest substrate temperature recorded was 34.5 ◦C in the 
AV60:40 T2 substrate, followed closely by AV50:50 T1 at 34 ◦C, 
AV60:40 T1 at 33.5 ◦C, and AV50:50 T2 at 31.5 ◦C, all observed between 
days 10 and 11 of the experiment. In research by Bonelli et al., a constant 
reactor temperature of 27◦± 0.5 ◦C was maintained, but the substrate 
temperatures increased to 40 ◦C (Bonelli et al., 2019). Kim et al. re­
ported that temperatures can rise up to 45 ◦C in black soldier fly larvae 
due to enzyme production for protein degradation (Kim et al., 2011) 
Similarly, Parra Paz et al. suggested that larvae tend to cluster within the 
substrate, which raises the temperature due to overcrowding and 
movement (Parra Paz et al., 2015).
The moisture in the growth chamber remained consistently between 
50 % and 65 % during the first two weeks, but decreased to 35 % in the 
third week. However, this decrease in moisture in the growth chamber 
did not appear to affect the moisture content in the substrates, which 
ended with an average of 66 %.
3.5.2. Gas sensors
The bioreactor was programmed to aerate the growth chamber every 
60 min by running the fans for five minutes. During the first 24 h, the 
average CO2 concentration was 3394 ppm, while the air quality index 
was 285 ppm. The highest concentration recorded was 18 433 ppm CO2 
on day 4, and 466 ppm for air quality on day 21 of the experiment 
(Fig. 6). After day 11, the CO2 concentration remained in the upper 
detection range. Although the recommended CO2 concentration for 
humans in closed enclosures is less than 800 ppm (C. Wang et al., 2024), 
this study cannot determine whether the CO2 concentration had any 
impact on the low growth of larvae fed with agave bagasse and vinasse. 
However, it may serve as an adjustment factor for aeration in future 
experiments.
4. Conclusions
The bioreactor, designed for controlling and monitoring environ­
mental variables and self-developed gases, was programmed with open- 
source software. It was developed and tested through an experiment that 
involved feeding black soldier fly larvae with waste cattle manure, sheep 
manure, and mixtures of agave bagasse and vinasse. The trials were 
conducted with a growth chamber temperature set between 27 ◦C and 
29 ◦C, and relative humidity maintained between 50 % and 65 %. The 
larvae fed with manure exhibited growth similar to that reported by 
other authors using the same diet.
The growth chamber was programmed to aerate every 60 min for 5 
min; however, the CO2 concentration exceeded the levels recommended 
for human occupancy in closed environments. This result can be utilized 
to establish aeration conditions based on the CO2 concentration in the 
air. Additionally, the system enabled the monitoring of substrate tem­
perature changes and demonstrated a low incidence of relative humidity 
affecting the moisture content in the substrates.
The system allowed access to recorded data and adjustments of pa­
rameters in the controller. The reactor was designed with a scalability 
approach for areas with low temperatures or significant seasonal tem­
perature changes, ensuring the maintenance of a continuous process. 
The results generated in this study can be applied to biomass production, 
the creation of by-products, and the investigation of growth responses to 
various substrates and environmental factors in controlled environ­
ments. Furthermore, this study facilitated the collection of fundamental 
data for the development of mathematical models, which will be the 
focus of future work.
The use of Hermetia illucens and bioreactors for managing and 
valorizing organic and agro-industrial waste represents an innovative 
and promising approach that combines effective waste reduction with 
the production of high-value biomass. However, the large-scale imple­
mentation of these technologies faces significant challenges. One key 
concern is energy consumption associated with the control of critical 
parameters such as temperature, humidity, and ventilation in bio­
reactors, which raises questions about the sustainability of the process. 
While the use of sensors and automated systems enhances precision and 
control, it also potentially increases indirect carbon emissions due to the 
energy required to operate these devices.
